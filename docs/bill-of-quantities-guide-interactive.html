01

What the Bill of Quantities is

Quantities from the model. Prices from the catalog. Gaps in plain sight.

A Bill of Quantities answers a deceptively simple question: “what is in this part of the network, and what would it cost?” VoltEdge builds it directly from the live model — no spreadsheet, no separate asset register. You pick a scope (what to count), the tool rolls the scoped features up into quantities, multiplies each quantity by a catalog rate, and hands back a grouped, totalled schedule you can read on screen or export. Two ideas are worth holding from the start:

  • Quantities come from the model; prices come from the catalog. Quantities are never guessed — a conductor’s length is measured from its geometry, a transformer is counted as one item. What turns a quantity into money is a rate someone entered. No rate, no price.
  • A costing gap is shown, never hidden. A scoped feature with no applicable rate does not silently become zero. It appears in a separate Unpriced section with the quantity it would have contributed and the reason no rate applied. The grand total is therefore always honest: the sum of what could be priced, with the gaps listed beside it.

The tool is read-only and read-scoped: it can only price what you are allowed to see. 🗺️ If your territory access hides a feeder, it is absent from your BoQ too. Pricing a scope

▶ the estimator’s workflow — five steps.

🔒 Needs features:read — anyone who can read the map. Tom has been asked for a rough materials cost of everything on Feeder 7. Here is the whole workflow, on a working copy of the panel.

  1. Open the panel. On the map toolbar, press the Bill of Quantities button (the calculator icon). A floating panel opens; it can be moved and resized, and stays put as you pan.
  2. Choose a scope. The Scope dropdown offers four ways to say what to count (table below).
  3. Press Price. The schedule appears: priced lines grouped by class, each class subtotalled, under a single grand total. Any Unpriced buckets are listed in amber.
  4. Switch currency (optional). Once a result exists, a Currency dropdown lists every configured currency. The schedule re-prices instantly — the conversion happens server-side off the same base figures, so it never changes what was counted, only how it reads (§5.2).
  5. Export. PDF opens an A4 schedule in a preview dialog; CSV saves the spreadsheet-friendly form.

2.1 Reading the schedule

Each priced line reads Item · Qty · Unit · Rate · Total. Item is the grouping within a class — its subtype and/or material (e.g. Underground · XLPE 185 mm²), or (all) when a single class-default rate covers everything. Qty is the rolled-up quantity (a count for discrete assets, a length in km for linear ones); Unit is each or km; Rate is the unit price in the shown currency; Total is Qty × Rate. The footer reports the priced-line count and names the currency (and the base when you are viewing another). The Unpriced block lists each gap with its quantity, unit and a plain-language reason — the administrator’s to-do list.

code-verified What a scope actually counts

The scope is only one of several filters applied to every class. The engine counts a feature only when all of these hold, and a feature that fails one simply isn’t in the schedule — it is not “Unpriced”:

  • Not soft-deleted (deleted_at IS NULL).
  • Readable by you — 🗺️ territory read-enforcement and the private-drafts rule apply: a never-published feature authored by someone else is invisible (switch to Dana to see spare record K-04 appear). This is why two people can price “the whole network” and get different totals.
  • Placed, for linear classes. A conductor with no geometry contributes zero length, a zero-quantity bucket is dropped, and it appears nowhere — neither priced nor Unpriced. (C-009 is such a span.)
  • In a compiled class. A class not yet compiled has no table to count; it is skipped silently. An unknown class is skipped too.
  • Locatable, for spatial scopes. Territory, radius and bbox scopes skip classes with no geometry (like the spare-equipment register) rather than erroring — so a Territory BoQ omits them, while Whole network includes them.

If an explicit feature list (trace result or selection) is supplied it takes precedence and defines the classes; the other filters above still apply to those features. Try Territory vs Whole network in the panel and read the spare-register line.

code-verified The two exports, precisely

CSV — header Class, Subtype, Material, Basis, Quantity, Unit, Rate (CUR), Total (CUR), Status; one row per priced line (Status = priced), then one per unpriced item (Status = unpriced: <reason>, rate/total blank, material shown as its code), ending in a Grand total row. Note the CSV has no column marking an overridden line — a line priced off a per-feature override is just priced (only the on-screen schedule flags it). PDF — an A4 schedule with a title block (scope, currency, date), the grouped table with subtotals and grand total, the Unpriced section and a page footer; the dialog’s Print and Download buttons send it to a printer or save it, Close dismisses it with neither. Press the buttons in the panel above to see both.

03

The cost model

∑ how a quantity becomes a number.

Administrators need this chapter to know where to enter a rate; estimators can read it to understand why a line priced the way it did. Every feature class is priced one of two ways, decided by its geometry: line classes by length, everything else by count.

3.1 Linear classes — priced per kilometre

A class drawn as a line (a conductor, a cable) is priced by length. The length is a true geodesic distance — ST_Length over the geometry transformed to a geography — so it is metres on the ground, not degrees, summed and divided into kilometres. The rate for that length is resolved in this order:

  1. The feature’s material, via its material coded field. If the feature records a material whose material spec (/admin/materials) carries a cost_per_km, that is the rate. The preferred path: two spans of the same class but different conductor price differently, correctly.
  2. A per_km rate from the price book, as a fallback when the material carries no per-km cost. code-verified This lookup is subtype-aware, exactly like the discrete one: a per_km row for the span’s subtype wins, else the class-default per_km row. (The original manual only mentioned a “class-level” fallback; you can price Overhead and Underground differently here too.)

If neither resolves, the length is unpriced. 💡 This is why conductor prices live on their material specs, not only in the price book: a conductor’s cost is a property of the cable you chose. The class-level per_km rows are the backstop for lines you don’t vary by material.

3.2 Discrete classes — priced ‘each’

A class that is a point, a polygon, or has no geometry at all (a transformer, a switch, a compound, a register entry) is priced per item from the unit-rate price book (metadata.cost_rates), keyed by feature class + subtype. A rate with a specific subtype prices only features of that subtype; a rate with no subtype is the class default, covering every subtype that has no more specific rate of its own. A discrete feature whose class has neither a matching-subtype rate nor a class default is unpriced.

3.3 Per-feature overrides — the exception that wins

A per-feature override (Chapter 6) pins an exact unit cost to one feature, in the base currency, in that feature’s natural unit (per km for a line, each for a discrete asset). An overridden feature is pulled out of the normal roll-up and priced at its override — and is therefore never unpriced. Everything else in its class still prices off the catalog.

3.4 Precedence, in one line

per-feature override ▸ else material cost_per_km (linear) / subtype rate (discrete) ▸ else class default (per_km fallback / default each rate) ▸ else Unpriced.

3.5 A rate can sit in the book and be ignored code-verified

The unit basis must match the class’s geometry. A line class reads only per_km rows; every other class reads only each rows. A row of the wrong basis is not an error and not a warning — the engine skips it as if it did not exist, and the feature lands in Unpriced with a reason that says “no rate is set”. The Add-rate form defaults to each, so adding a “default” rate to a conductor class without changing the basis quietly does nothing.

It gets one step worse: the price book allows one row per (class, subtype) regardless of basis (the unique index is on class + subtype only), so you cannot “fix” a wrong-basis class default by adding a second row — the form blocks it as a duplicate. The fix is to edit the existing row’s unit basis (the form allows it). Try it in Interactive 4: select a transformer, and in the price book (Chapter 5) change a rate’s basis.

If the unpriced reason says…The fix
“no 'each' rate is set for this class/subtype”Add an each rate for the class (default or that subtype) — or check an existing row isn’t per_km.
“no material is selected and no per_km rate is set for this class”Set the feature’s material, or add a per_km rate (class default or this subtype) — check an existing row isn’t each.
“the selected material has no cost_per_km, and no per_km rate is set for this class”Set cost_per_km on the material spec, or add a per_km rate as a fallback.

3.6 Material grouping applies to every class code-verified

A schedule line is a (subtype, material) bucket. For a class that has a material-backed coded field — the first active field whose domain contains at least one material-backed value, one per class — the roll-up splits by that material even for a discrete class, where the material has no effect on the rate (it is priced by subtype only). So a class of equipment with a “model” material field can show several lines at the same unit rate. A class with no such field shows (all) or just the subtype.

3.7 How the numbers are rounded code-verified

  • Quantities are rounded to 3 decimals for display (a length in km is to the nearest metre).
  • The engine totals every line from the unrounded quantity × base rate, accumulating the base-currency figure (total_base).
  • Each displayed rate and line total is converted (× per-base) and rounded to 2 decimals individually. The grand total is converted and rounded once, from the unrounded base total.
  • So the displayed lines can sum to a cent or two different from the grand total — the grand total is the more accurate figure. The panel footer in Interactive 2 flags the difference when it happens. Lines are sorted by class code (not display name), then subtype, then material.

04

Administering currencies 🔒

One base currency; everything else is presentation.

🔒 The cost catalog is admin-only to read as well as write — the /admin screens inherit the metadata:write gate. If you can’t see /admin/cost-catalog, you lack the permission. Ada administers the catalog at Admin → Cost catalog. The page has two cards — Currencies and the Unit-rate price book — plus pointers out to material specs and the per-feature overrides.

4.1 The base currency

VoltEdge stores every cost — catalog rates, material per-km costs, overrides — in one base currency. This is the single source of truth; every other currency is a presentation layer computed from it. Exactly one currency is flagged base; its conversion factor is pinned to 1 (the form forces and locks that), it cannot be deleted, and the system enforces that there is always exactly one (a partial unique index plus a check constraint). 💡 Choose the base currency deliberately and early — it is the denomination in which you will actually type every rate. Seeded installs start with USD.

4.2 Adding a presentation currency

To let estimators read schedules in Ghana Cedi, Ada presses Add currency and enters a Code (fixed once created), a Name, and Per base unit — how many of this currency equal one base unit (if one USD is worth 12 GHS, the factor is 12; it must be > 0). The BoQ’s Currency dropdown then offers GHS, and choosing it multiplies every base figure by 12. ⚠️ These are static factors, not a live FX feed — update them when your rates move. Deleting a presentation currency leaves the base-denominated prices untouched; schedules simply can no longer be shown in it. An unknown currency in an API request is a 400.

05

Administering the price book 🔒

The backbone that turns counts — and un-material’d lengths — into money.

The Unit-rate price book card lists the each/per_km rates keyed by class and subtype.

5.1 Adding a rate

Ada presses Add rate and fills: Feature class (fixed once created); Subtype — Class default (all subtypes) or a specific subtype; Unit basis — each (per item) or per_km (per km of length); Unit rate — the price in the base currency (must be ≥ 0); Notes — free text (e.g. a supplier quote reference). The uniqueness rule is one rate per (class, subtype) — including one class-default row per class — and, as §3.5 explained, that holds across both bases. The form blocks a duplicate.

💡 Where to put conductor costs. For conductors you price by material, don’t add a class per_km rate — set cost_per_km on the material spec (/admin/materials) so each cable prices correctly. Use a class per_km rate only for linear classes you don’t vary by material (or as the backstop).

5.2 How currency conversion interacts with rates

Every rate you type is in the base currency. The estimator’s currency dropdown never changes a stored rate — it reconverts the whole already-computed schedule for display. Flip “View rates in” above: the stored value does not move. A rate is entered once, in base, and read in any currency.

5.3 Closing the Unpriced gap

The fastest way to drive the catalog to completeness is to run a whole-network BoQ and read the Unpriced section (the right-hand panel above is exactly that, live). Each entry names a class (and subtype/material) with no rate. Add the missing rate, re-price, and watch the entry move up into the priced schedule. An empty Unpriced section means full costing coverage for that scope. ⚠️ Deleting a rate doesn’t error loudly — features it used to price simply revert to unpriced until another rate covers them. The confirmation dialog says exactly this.

Run the trap (scenario ②). It adds a per_km row to the isolator subtype — the kind of mistake the form allows. The row appears in the book, but the live panel still lists isolators as Unpriced, and the row is flagged ignored. Scenario ③ fixes it the right way: edit the row’s basis to each.

Chapter summary

  • One rate per (class, subtype) across both bases; a class default covers subtypes without their own rate.
  • The unit basis must match the geometry (line → per_km, else → each) or the row is ignored.
  • Rates are typed in base; deleting one quietly reverts features to Unpriced. Use whole-network BoQ as the to-do list.
06

Per-feature overrides 🔒

When one real asset cost something the book can’t express.

🔒 Needs metadata:write. The override controls are hidden entirely from users without it. Dana sets a per-feature override without touching the catalog everyone else prices from.

▶ Steps. Open the feature on the map, go to its Materials tab, and find the Cost override section. Press Set override, enter the unit cost (shown in the base currency, in the feature’s natural unit — per km for a line, each for a discrete asset), and save. An amber badge marks the feature as overridden. Change edits it; the reset/clear action removes it and returns the feature to normal catalog pricing.

∑ What it does. The override is stored against that one feature (metadata.feature_cost_overrides, one per feature), in the base currency, and must be ≥ 0. At pricing time the feature is excluded from its class’s normal roll-up and priced at the override instead. code-verified Overridden features are then collapsed by (subtype, material, rate): two features at the same hand-set rate share one flagged line; different rates make separate lines. A linear override is the rate × that feature’s own length; an unplaced conductor (zero length) is skipped even when overridden. The override ignores the unit-basis rules of §3.5 — it prices whatever the feature’s natural unit is. And the same visibility filters (territory, drafts, deleted) still apply.

07

Beyond the panel

The API, the agent tool, and what the UI doesn’t surface yet.

  • Pricing from an agent. The BoQ engine is exposed as the run_bill_of_quantities MCP tool (gated features:read), so an AI assistant can price a scope and read back the same grouped schedule the panel shows. The catalog-administration side (currencies, rates, overrides) is not in MCP — it stays a deliberate admin-screen action.
  • Scopes the panel doesn’t surface yet. The engine also supports radius and bounding-box spatial scopes. They are reachable through the API but not wired into the panel, because they need a map draw/point gesture that isn’t built. The four scopes in Chapter 2 are what the UI offers today.
  • What’s planned, not built. Server-side CSV/XLSX export and a per-assembly Bill of Materials are the next slice; today’s exports are the client-side PDF and CSV in Chapter 2.
