∑ One structural fact first

The LV and metering classes are not defined in a migration — they’re authored as seed metadata (seed/*.sql) and then compiled into physical gis.<class> tables (task compile CODE=…). Migrations only create the generic machinery (the feature_classes / fields / terminals / network_rules tables, new relationship kinds). So “which migration adds LV_CONDUCTOR?” has no answer — it’s seed/lv_conductors_example.sql. The migrations that do matter are the relationship-kind wideners (0080, 0121), called out where relevant.

01

The LV layer in one page

The same graph as MV, starting where MV ends.

VoltEdge models a network as a graph: edge features (conductors) joined at junction features (devices) through typed terminals, with every join authorised by a network rule. The LV layer is that same graph, starting at a distribution transformer’s secondary. Four ideas carry the chapter:

  • LV starts at the transformer’s T2. A DIST_XFMR has three terminals: T1 (primary, the MV side), T2 (secondary — the LV bus), and T3 (ground). Every LV feeder originates at a T2.
  • Conductors are edges; everything else is a node. LV_CONDUCTOR is an edge with no terminals — it’s the wire. Cut-outs, switches, pillars, meters and service points are junction nodes whose terminals power flows through or into. Chambers and pillars are containers — they hold things; they’re not on the electrical path.
  • Phase is data, not structure. A conductor doesn’t come in “single-phase” and “three-phase” classes; it carries a phase field. Some connections are phase-gated (§8).
  • The meter is optional and the service point is the leaf. What always terminates an LV supply is a SERVICE_POINT (the point of delivery) or an unmetered leaf like a STREET_LIGHT.

From those four ideas the model authorises exactly seven valid supply chains (§8). Click any part of the tour below to see its class.

02

The LV conductor

The wire every supply rides.

LV_CONDUCTOR is drawn as a map line, network_role = edge, and carries no terminals — an edge is joined to nodes; it doesn’t own join points. Three fields are required on every span:

FieldDomainWhat it is
phasePHASEWhich phases the span carries — L1L2L3N for a three-phase main, L1N for a single-phase service, etc.
conductor_specLV_CABLE_SPECThe cable type — resolves to real impedance/ampacity for load flow and to a per-km cost for the Bill of Quantities.
lv_roleLV_ROLEmain (the backbone down a street) or service (the drop to one premises).
Tip — lv_role is a field, not a subtype, on purpose
An abandoned design split LV into separate LV_MAIN and LV_SERVICE classes; the model instead keeps one class and makes main-vs-service an attribute, so a main re-tasked as a service is an edit, not a re-creation. The subtypes encode construction instead: 1 = overhead_lv_conductor, 2 = underground_lv_conductor.

The cable catalog, in your hands

Each conductor_spec points at an LV_CABLE_SPEC coded value backed by a material_specs row. Pick one, set a length and a load, and see what the numbers on that row do to the voltage at the far end. (The arithmetic is the standard line-drop approximation — a teaching aid; the load-flow engine is authoritative.)

Where a span originates — and what’s phase-gated

The rule DIST_XFMR T2 → LV_CONDUCTOR is the LV source hop. ⚠️ Once an LV conductor exists on a transformer, the direct DIST_XFMR T2 → SERVICE_POINT rule is retired (is_valid=false) — supply must flow through the conductor, not leap from the transformer to a customer.

The LV_CONDUCTOR → LV_CONDUCTOR splice rule is phase-gated (phase_rule = phases_intersect): a single-phase L1N service can tap a three-phase L1L2L3N main (they share line phase L1) but two services on different phases can’t be spliced together by mistake. Both endpoints must carry a phase — a missing one is a hard error, not a silent pass. And neutral is deliberately ignored: L1N and L2N both contain an N, but they’re on different phases and are not connectable. Test it:

03

LV devices: cut-outs, switches, pillars

Between the main and the customer. All are junction nodes.

Service cut-out — the intake fuse

SERVICE_CUTOUT. Subtypes 1 = fuse_carrier, 2 = switch_fuse. T1 (line) / T2 (load) and a device_state; an OPEN cut-out is a trace barrier — a trace stops at a pulled fuse exactly as the network does.

LV switch — the generic switch

LV_SWITCH. Geometry-optional inline switch, one subtype standard, device_state plus optional protection attributes (is_fused, fuse_rating_a, curve_ref). OPEN is a barrier.

Link box / feeder pillar — the flat junction

LV_LINK_BOX. Subtypes 1 = feeder_pillar, 2 = link_box. One incoming T1 and three switchable outgoing ways T2/T3/T4.

LV pillar — the assembly

LV_PILLAR is a container (network LV) — the LV sibling of the RMU. You place it as an assembly (🔒 features:write + relationships:commit) and it fans out its internals in one transaction.

Honest limitation
The link box’s OPEN barrier is feature-level, not per-way. A normally-open way is modelled by the absence of a connection on that terminal, not by a per-way open flag. If you need true per-way isolation, use the LV pillar instead.

Place a pillar, commission it way by way

An assembly fans out one LV_BUSBAR and N LV_FUSEs: WAYS_4 → 1× busbar + 4× fuse, WAYS_6 → 1× busbar + 6× fuse. Each LV_FUSE is a diagram-only node (geometry_type=none) with T1 (bus side) / T2 (out), a fuse_rating (100A…400A, BS88) and a device_state. The fuses are born OPEN — a pillar is commissioned way by way. LV_FUSE OPEN is a per-way barrier. Compare it with the link box below.

04

Service points: the point of delivery

The leaf of an LV supply.

SERVICE_POINT is a junction with a single terminal T1, drawn as a violet diamond.

  • Subtypes = customer class: 1 = residential, 2 = commercial, 3 = industrial.
  • Load fields: demand_kw, power_factor (unset = unity), and customers_served (integer; NULL reads as 1 — it’s the SAIDI/SAIFI denominator).
  • 💡 No phase field. Phase lives on the conductor and the meter, not the leaf. And the old meter_number scalar is gone — the meter is a first-class METER feature linked by ownership (Chapter 5), not a string on the service point.
  • Fed by …→SERVICE_POINT T1 from the conductor, SERVICE_CUTOUT T2, or METER T2 — the last of which is what makes it a metered leaf.
Ownership says who bills. Connectivity says where the current flows. The model keeps them apart on purpose.

That distinction is the subject of the next chapter’s first widget.

05

LV metering: the revenue meter

∑ The heart of the guide — and one physical fact drives all of it.

At LV the meter is inline — the customer’s current physically flows through it — so METER is a real junction node with T1 (line) and T2 (load). That single fact drives the whole LV metering model, and it’s the sharpest contrast with MV metering (Chapter 6), where current never flows through the meter at all.

The meter’s record

The subtype encodes the metering arrangement: 1 = whole_current (the meter carries full load current) and 2 = ct_metered (it reads a current transformer’s 5 A secondary). ct_ratio is the model’s canonical example of a per-subtype-required field: physically nullable on the class, but made mandatory for ct_metered by a single subtype_field_rules row. The SP-owns-METER model

A service point owns its meter through a composite (ownership) relationship, one_to_many: 0..N meters per service point; each meter belongs to at most one service point (enforced in api/internal/relationship/cardinality.go). That’s why a service point can carry a main meter and a check meter, a multi-rate pair, or no meter at all, without any of it being a magic string.

Note the two relationships a meter is in: owned by a service point (the commercial/billing fact) and wired inline on the electrical path. They usually point at the same service point but are modelled separately on purpose. Toggle the layers, then try breaking the cardinality:

The meter chamber and the busbar fan-out

Several meters in one cabinet (an apartment block) use two more classes. METER_CHAMBER is the physical enclosure — a container (not on the electrical path, no terminals), subtypes cabinet/room, carrying a chamber_ref asset tag. It contains its busbar and meters (containment) and owns a CHAMBER_SCHEMATIC diagram restricted to exactly {LV_BUSBAR, METER}. LV_BUSBAR is the distribution board inside it: T1 is the incoming supply; T2 is the equipotential bar that fans out to every tenant meter.

∑ Why one terminal feeds twenty meters

A busbar is a solid bar — every tap is the same electrical node. So instead of modelling twenty switchable ways, the model uses one rule, LV_BUSBAR T2 → METER T1, and lets it be satisfied N times — one runtime.feature_connectivity row per meter on the same T2. Twenty flats need no twenty-terminal schema, just twenty connections on the one bar.

Unmetered supplies and CT metering

STREET_LIGHT is an unmetered leaf — subtypes column_mounted/wall_mounted, a luminaire_type (LED/SON/SOX/MH), fed by a phase-gated rule LV_CONDUCTOR → STREET_LIGHT T1 (phases_intersect) so a light only hangs off a phase the conductor actually carries. And CT metering at LV is an attribute, not a class — the ct_metered subtype plus the ct_ratio field on the ordinary meter. (That changes at MV — next chapter.)

06

MV metering: a different shape, for a reason

∑ The physics that forces a different model.

At MV the primary current is far too large to run through a meter, so it never does. The meter instead reads the secondaries of instrument transformers — a CT steps the line current down to 5 A, a VT steps the voltage down to 110 V, and the meter multiplies those back up. So an MV meter is off the primary path entirely. Modelling it as an inline METER would be a lie about where the current goes.

So MV metering mirrors the LV SERVICE_POINT + METER split, but with the meter off-path:

  • MV_METERING_POINT — the delivery-point leaf, the commercial boundary where the utility hands off to the customer; a “trace to the customer” ends here. A junction with a single T1, subtypes industrial/commercial_complex/bulk_supply, the required supply_voltage (11KV/33KV) and contracted_demand_kva, plus optional tariff_ref/account_ref.
  • MV_METER — the meter device, network_role = none, no terminals (like a pole — an asset, not a path node). Subtypes main_meter/check_meter; required meter_serial and metering_class (IEC 62053: 0.2S/0.5S/1.0). It carries no CT/VT ratio of its own — it reads them from the linked instrument transformers.
  • MV_METERING_CUBICLE — the enclosure, a container (indoor/outdoor_kiosk) that contains {MV_METER, MV_CT, MV_VT}.
07

Network metering: energy accounting

The third kind of meter has no customer at all.

NETWORK_METER (migration 0121) meters a point of the network itself — a substation incomer, a feeder head, a transformer’s output, a bus section, or a tie between territories — so the utility can account for energy.

  • network_role = none, no terminals, no network rules, no trace barrier. It is an accounting overlay, not a topology node — a trace never walks through it. “What’s downstream of this meter?” is answered by tracing the element it meters, not the meter.
  • Subtypes = accounting purpose: substation_incomer, feeder_head, transformer_metering, bus_section, boundary_exchange, interchange_tie, generation_export.
  • Fields: required meter_serial + metering_class; optional ct_ratio, vt_ratio (11KV_110/33KV_110) and external_ref — the join key into the external MDM/head-end.
It stores no readings
kWh / kVArh / demand live in the external metering system; VoltEdge holds only the asset and the external_ref to fetch them at view time. The class is a registry of where energy is accounted, not a store of how much.

It attaches through a metering_accounting relationship (migration 0121, many_to_many) onto the metered element — DIST_XFMR, POWER_XFMR, MV_BUSBAR, MV_CONDUCTOR or LV_CONDUCTOR. Try to trace through one:

08

How it all connects: the rules and the seven chains

Memorise these and you know what the model will — and won’t — let you build.

Every LV connection is authorised by a row in metadata.network_rules (from-class [+ terminal] → to-class [+ terminal], with an optional phase_rule). The LV rule set authorises exactly seven valid supply chains:

#ChainUse
1LV_CONDUCTOR → SERVICE_POINTbare / unmetered tap
2LV_CONDUCTOR → SERVICE_CUTOUT → SERVICE_POINTfused, unmetered
3LV_CONDUCTOR → METER → SERVICE_POINTmetered, no cut-out
4LV_CONDUCTOR → SERVICE_CUTOUT → METER → SERVICE_POINTthe standard house
5LV_CONDUCTOR → LV_BUSBAR → METER×N → SERVICE_POINT×Nboard fan-out
6LV_CONDUCTOR → SERVICE_CUTOUT → LV_BUSBAR → METER×N → SERVICE_POINT×Nthe apartment block
7LV_CONDUCTOR → STREET_LIGHTunmetered leaf

Two rules are phase-gated (phases_intersect): the conductor-to-conductor splice and the conductor-to-street-light hop. Everywhere else a phase mismatch is allowed because the device handles it. And remember the retired rule: once an LV conductor exists, the direct transformer-to-service-point hop is invalid.

Build a supply against that rule set yourself. Each button is a real attempt; the engine below accepts or rejects it the way the rule table would.

09

Reading consumption off the LV network

▶ How much load, how many customers, at what priority, sits below here?

The service / consumption rollup (api/internal/trace/service.go) answers it — not with a power-flow solve, but by summing the planner’s stored nameplate figures across the load leaves a trace reaches. What counts as a load leaf: SERVICE_POINT always, and a METER only when its meter_role = PRIMARY. A CHECK meter or a role-less device meter contributes nothing.

What it computes: per set of reached leaves it sums service_points, customers (NULL customers_served = 1), demand_kw and demand_kva — where kVA = kW ÷ power_factor per leaf (pf defaults to unity), summed, not re-derived off an averaged power factor. It also buckets load by restoration tier (LOAD_PRIORITY P1…P4; P1 “Life support”, P2 “Essential”; “critical” = P1 or P2).

A subtlety worth seeing in a real tool
MTR-A and MTR-B in the seed have no meter_role set, so under the Chapter 9 PRIMARY contract the meters are inert — the 45 kW rolls up via SP-A/SP-B, not the meters. 10.2 A closed LV loop (the ring network)

The LV ring is the one closed-loop LV example: two mains from one transformer meeting at a single meter (MTR-R → SP-R, commercial · 30 kW · pf 1.0). LVB is exactly twice LVA’s length, so twice its resistance. Current splits inverse to resistance, so of SP-R’s 30 kW, LVA carries 20 kW and LVB carries 10 kW. pf is unity on purpose so kW = kVA and the arithmetic is clean. Move the lengths and watch the split follow — the default is the seed’s 2 : 1.

10.3 The apartment block (chain #6)

A twenty-flat block is chain #6: one SERVICE_CUTOUT off the street main, into one LV_BUSBAR inside one METER_CHAMBER, with twenty METER → SERVICE_POINT pairs hanging off the one T2 bar. You build it by placing the chamber, dropping the busbar and meters on its CHAMBER_SCHEMATIC diagram, and letting the single LV_BUSBAR T2 → METER T1 rule be satisfied twenty times. Each flat’s meter is owned by its own service point; all twenty are contained in the one chamber. (Slide it to 20 in Interactive 7.)

11

Asset ownership: utility vs private

Declare private at the boundary; it propagates downstream.

Not every asset on the map belongs to the utility. A common arrangement: the utility supplies and meters an MV bulk supply (an MV_METERING_POINT of subtype bulk_supply), and the customer owns everything below it — their own distribution transformer and LV network. The ownership axis captures that: every transformer and LV-set class carries a coded ownership field — utility (the default; unset reads as utility) or private.

Mark the customer’s DIST_XFMR private, and everything electrically downstream is private too.
∑ Why propagation, not per-feature tagging

Ownership is a connectivity property: an asset is the customer’s if it hangs off the customer’s transformer. So the propagation is a downstream walk that ignores open switches (a normally-open tie inside the private network doesn’t change who owns what lies beyond it) and crosses the transformer into the LV (the whole galvanic island below the boundary is private). It is not an energisation trace.

🔒 Needs features:write. Open the customer’s transformer’s actions menu (⌄). On a transformer it carries two actions: Mark downstream private (declares it private and stamps the whole downstream LV network in one transaction) and Re-sync ownership (recomputes the whole map from every currently-private transformer — the drift fix after a re-wire). How private assets read

  • On the map: private plant looks exactly like every other feature — standard colours and symbology. Ownership is a data flag, not a restyle. (The tinted nodes above are a teaching aid.)
  • In the feature panel: a private feature shows a small “Privately owned” chip; a transformer (the declare boundary) carries the two actions. A plain utility feature shows no ownership UI at all — utility is the silent default.
  • In a trace / load flow: a trace does not stop at the ownership boundary — it crosses and includes the private section — but every private feature in the result carries a “Private” badge (and the load-flow table an Owner column). The deliberate “include it, but flag it” behaviour.
  • From an agent: mark_ownership_downstream_private and resync_ownership do the same two actions over the MCP surface.
Tip
You can set ownership by hand on a single feature (it’s a normal attribute edit) — and that’s exactly how drift starts. The Mark downstream action is the intended workflow.

What’s deliberately not (yet) modelled

Who owns it

v1 is a plain utility/private flag, not a named organisation. A richer “owned by Acme Steelworks” layer (CIM AssetOwner) can come later.

The boundary

Declared on the transformer today. A private MV network above it would want the boundary at the MV intake / metering point — a fast follow; the engine already handles any start feature.

Load flow

Treats the private site’s downstream as ordinary network for now; the trace family tags ownership, but the power-flow bus/branch tables don’t yet.
