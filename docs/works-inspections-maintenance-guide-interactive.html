About this manual

The how-to companion for the Works area — the Works page in the sidebar and the Works tab on any asset. It covers Works (defects and work orders), Inspections (reusable checklists that turn findings into tracked defects) and Maintenance (those parts run on a cycle). It documents what has been built: most importantly, recurring, auto-generated maintenance plans are not built yet (Chapter 6). If you administer VoltEdge see the Admin & Break-Glass Guide; for the map and the rest of the product, the User Handbook.

01

The WAM mental model

Three record types, one closed loop, two permissions.

1.1 What WAM is — and what it is not

VoltEdge’s core is a map of the network: every pole, transformer, switch and cable is a feature with a geometry and electrical intelligence. WAM sits alongside it and records the operational life of those assets — the problems found on them, the jobs done to them, and the routine checks run against them.

  • A transformer is a GIS feature — location, terminals, rated kVA, connections; it appears in traces and analyses.
  • A defect on that transformer (“oil weeping at the LV bushing”) is not a feature. It’s an operational record about it, pointing at it by reference. You never “draw” a defect or a work order — you log it against an asset.
If it exists in the world and connects to other things, it’s a feature. If it’s something that happened to or needs to happen to a feature, it’s a WAM record.

1.2 The three record types

Defect

“What is wrong.”

One health finding on one asset: a severity, a source, a short status lifecycle. The module’s inbox.

Work order

“The job that gets done.”

One typed engine for inspection, corrective, preventive and construction work — one lifecycle, a priority, an asset, an assignee, a schedule.

Inspection

“The routine check.”

A reusable template (checklist) and a filled-out inspection of it against an asset. A failing item can be armed to auto-log a defect.

1.3 The closed loop

Findings become tracked problems; problems become planned work; completed work and fresh inspections reveal the next findings. A defect enters by hand, from an OMS outage, or automatically from a failing inspection item. From an open defect you raise a corrective work order in one action; the crew completes it; the defect is resolved.

The habit this manual teaches
Don’t resolve a defect by fixing it quietly. Raise a work order from it, do the work against that order, complete the order, then resolve the defect — leaving a linked finding → job → done trail an audit, a reliability report, or next year’s engineer can read.

1.4 Where WAM lives — two front doors

The Works page (the board) is the cross-asset view for planning and triage: a KPI strip (Open defects, Active orders, In progress, Unassigned), a defect → order flow board (inbox on the left, orders in flight on the right), and the inspection templates and history below, with each row’s actions in a compact ⋯ menu. The Works tab on an asset’s panel is the single-asset view — only that transformer’s or pole’s records, with the asset already filled in when you log. It appears on any asset, electrically connected or not. Same records, two angles — try both:

02

Defects: recording what’s wrong

The module’s atom — everything downstream is only as good as the defects feeding it.

2.1 What a defect records

  • An anchor — an asset (feature class + id), or a map location (an “orphan”). Never half of one (a class with no id) and never neither — VoltEdge rejects those.
  • A title (required) — what shows in every list. A severity (low/medium/high/critical, default medium). A source (inspection/outage/condition_monitoring/ad_hoc, default ad_hoc). A status (starts open).
  • Optional: a free-text defect type, description, notes, reported by/at, and for outage-sourced defects the OMS incident it explains.
Tip
“Corrosion” is a bad title; “Heavy corrosion on the HV earth strap, ~30% section loss” is a good one. Put the actionable specifics in the title, the evidence in the description.

2.2 The four ways a defect is born

1 · Board

Works → Log defect. Pick the asset class + id. Dana’s usual door for second-hand reports.

2 · Works tab

On the map, asset in view: the asset is already filled in. Also where a defect can be orphaned to a location.

3 · OMS outage

An implicated asset leaves a durable record: source = outage, linked to the incident.

4 · Inspection

A failing armed checklist item auto-logs one: source = inspection, titled from the prompt, linked to the item (Chapter 5).

One inbox
However born, a defect lands in one place — the board’s Defects column and the asset’s Works tab. Source is a label on the defect, not a place it lives.

2.5 Logging a defect, step by step

Try the rules: leave the title empty; set an asset class without an id; submit with neither an asset nor a location. For an orphan, click the map to drop the pin.

2.4 The status lifecycle · 2.6 Triage · 2.7 Raise a work order

Four states: open → work_ordered → resolved / dismissed, reopenable back to open. Terminal moves stamp a resolved timestamp; reopening clears it. You can’t raise work for a closed defect — reopen it first. Triage means one of three decisions per open defect: needs a fix → raise a work order; fixed/not real → resolve or dismiss; needs watching → leave open with a note.

Raise work order is the hinge, and it’s one atomic action: it creates a corrective order linked to the defect, titled Repair: <defect title>, with priority derived from severity, carrying the asset (and any location pin — for an orphan, the only anchor the crew gets), and flips the defect to work_ordered — but only from open. A second order against an already-ordered defect is allowed; a resolved/dismissed one is refused. Pick a defect and drive it:

Don’t resolve quietly
Resolving the moment work is done in the field, without a work order, is quicker in the moment and destroys the trail — a year later a report can’t show what was done or by whom. Resolve after the order that fixed it completes.
Pitfalls
Vague titles bury problems. Severity inflation breaks triage. Don’t re-log a defect that’s already work-ordered (dismiss genuine duplicates). Orphan defects must be pinned. Log where you are — on the map with the asset in view, use its Works tab.
03

Work orders: the one typed engine

Not four systems — one record type, four flavours.

3.1–3.3 One engine, four flavours

Every unit of work is the same kind of record, distinguished by a work type: corrective (fix a known problem — born from a defect), inspection (a checklist round — fulfilled by recording an inspection), preventive (time-based servicing, no specific defect) and construction (new build). The type is guidance, not a gate — pick it so the work is findable and reportable. You get one place to look, one set of habits, and consistent reporting. An order carries a title, a type, a priority (default medium), a status, an optional primary asset (naming it puts the order on that asset’s Works tab), an optional assignment and schedule, auto-stamped started/completed times and a completion note.

3.4 The shared lifecycle

draft → scheduled → assigned → in_progress → completed, or cancelled any time before done. Three mechanics keep it from being bureaucratic: creation auto-advances (name an assignee → assigned; a schedule only → scheduled; neither → draft); timestamps keep themselves honest (started-at is set once; completed-at is set on completion and cleared if you move off completed); and you don’t have to walk every step — the board gives you Start, Complete, Cancel.

3.5–3.8 Priority, assignment, schedule — create one

  • Assignment — four owners, different behaviour. Internal worker (assigned_to_user_id, picked from everyone holding wam:write) — rides into their field pack. Internal crew (crew_id) — fans out to every member’s pack, and the picker shows each crew’s load. External subcontractor (subcontractor_id, a registered firm) — records responsibility only, no pack fan-out, because the firm works off-system. Free-text label (assigned_to) — an ad-hoc one-off, no account, no fan-out. ∑ Work reaches a phone only through an internal owner (a user or a crew); a subcontractor or label reaches no pack. Crews are managed/dispatched by the Outage board, subcontractors at Admin → Subcontractors (see the OMS guide’s crews & work chapter).
  • Raised from an outage. Dispatching a crew or tasking a subcontractor from an incident (with Raise restoration work order) creates one of these orders for you — crew- or subcontractor-owned, source=outage, linked by incident_id; a crew arriving on-site even flips its restoration orders to in_progress.
  • Scheduled-for vs due-by (API/agent): both are planning labels — they don’t alert. Scheduled and unassigned is a perfectly good state.

3.10–3.11 Driving an order, reading the board

Pitfalls

04

Inspection templates: reusable checklists

The fail-severity arm is the heart of it.

4.1–4.2 Why checklists, and what a template is made of

Templates beat paragraphs on consistency (everyone answers the same questions in the same order), completeness (the twelve things are listed), automation (a structured “fail” can raise a defect) and evidence (this asset, these criteria, this date). A template has a head — a stable code (MV_SWGR_ANNUAL), a name, a description and an optional asset class scope — and at least one ordered item: a prompt (phrase it so “pass” is the good answer), a response type, an optional fail severity and optional guidance. Items appear in the order you list them — put them in the sequence a person walks the asset.

4.3 The four response types

TypeThe inspector records…Use for
pass_failPass / Fail (or N/A)Most of a checklist — “Any corrosion on the tank?”
ratingA number on a scale (plus pass/fail)Graded condition — “Paintwork 1–5.”
numericA measured reading (plus pass/fail)“Earth resistance (Ω)”, “Oil temperature (°C)”
textFree text (plus pass/fail)“Nameplate serial”, “Observations”

Every type still has a pass/fail outcome — it’s that outcome, not the raw number, that drives the auto-defect and the overall result. The default is pass_fail.

4.4 The fail-severity arm

Each item’s fail severity is either unset (“None — informational”: a fail is recorded with a ✗ but raises no defect) or armed at low/medium/high/critical (a failing answer auto-logs a defect at that severity when the inspection is recorded — on the asset, source inspection, titled from the prompt, linked to the item). Authoring is largely deciding which items are armed, and how urgently — you are pre-deciding the triage of a whole class of future failures. Leave observational items unset so a checklist doesn’t spray low-value defects into the inbox.

The field-app UI defaults a new item to medium — armed
A sensible default, but you must deliberately choose “None (informational)” for observational items, or every one will raise a defect when failed. Review each item before saving.

4.6–4.9 Scope, versioning, retiring — and who can author

  • Scope documents intent and pre-fills the asset class at record time; leave it blank for a general checklist.
  • Templates are versioned, not edited. There is no “edit items.” When a recorded inspection saves, each answer snapshots the prompt and response type, so a completed inspection is a permanent standalone record. To revise: create a new template and retire the old one.
  • Retired hides a template from the record picker but keeps it, so every inspection ever recorded against it still reads correctly. Restore reverses it. Nothing is deleted.
  • Write is flat: authoring, retiring and restoring all sit under wam:write — the same permission that lets a field technician log a defect. (Chapter 8.)

Pitfalls
Arm deliberately — the UI defaults to armed. Phrase prompts so “pass” is good. Order items as you walk the asset. Use guidance to make judgements objective. Scope class-specific lists. Don’t over-arm — a checklist where every item is high floods triage on a bad day.
05

Recording inspections: executing a checklist

The single most valuable action in WAM — and the one field crews do most.

5.1 What recording does — atomically

  1. Writes the inspection and one result row per item (each snapshotting its prompt and type).
  2. For every item answered Fail that carries a fail severity, logs a defect (asset, source inspection, armed severity, titled from the prompt) and links it back onto the item result.
  3. Rolls up the overall result: pass unless any item failed (a conditional override exists for processes that need a middle ground).
  4. If it’s fulfilling a work order and you asked, marks that order completed with the inspection outcome as its note.

All of it succeeds or none of it does, and a device-minted id makes replays no-ops (§5.9). Pick a template, optionally the planned order it fulfils (which pre-fills the asset), fill the checklist and watch the live preview line — then save:

06

Maintenance: running it on a cycle

The most honest chapter: strong tools — and you supply the cadence.

6.1–6.2 What maintenance means here

Maintenance isn’t a fourth module — it’s the deliberate, scheduled use of the three record types in three modes: inspection rounds (inspection orders fulfilled by recorded inspections), preventive servicing (greasing, tightening, oil top-ups, vegetation — preventive orders) and corrective follow-through (fix what the rounds find). Chapter 1’s loop, run on a schedule, is a regime.

6.3 What’s automated, and what you drive by hand

A failing armed item raises a defect.Deciding the cadence. VoltEdge stores no maintenance interval.
Raise work order creates a linked corrective order and flips the defect.Creating each cycle’s inspection and preventive orders.
Status auto-advances at creation; timestamps stamp themselves.Remembering when a cycle is due — no reminders, alerts or notifications; due-by and scheduled-for are labels, not triggers.

Recurring, auto-generated plans are a planned capability, not a built one
Nothing in VoltEdge will create a work order on a timer. Treat it as a superb execution and record system whose cadence you own — assets silently fall out of cycle otherwise.

6.4–6.7 Run a cycle

The recipe: author scoped, armed checklists once; write the cadences down outside VoltEdge; each cycle create the work orders, pre-assigned and scheduled; crews execute (record inspections fulfilling their orders; Start/Complete preventive orders); the planner triages and corrects; then close the cycle and diarise the next. 6.5–6.6 Tracking without a scheduler: filter by work type (inspection/preventive), by open (empty = round done), read the KPIs as progress, let crews use Mine, and use scheduled-for/due-by as dates (they inform; they don’t alert). Maintenance plugs into asset tags (scan the asset, open its Works tab), the offline field pack, and OMS (outage-sourced defects land in the same inbox your rounds feed).

Pitfalls
The system won’t remind you — keep an external cadence document and diarise the next cycle every time you close one. A completed round isn’t a scheduled next one. Scope templates and assign at creation. Let rounds feed triage; don’t shadow-track findings. Use preventive vs corrective honestly. Due-by is a plan, not an alarm.
07

In the field, offline

The field app is a capture tool, not a second control room.

7.1–7.2 A pack and an outbox

The pack is a snapshot of the network and the Works context frozen when you build it; the outbox is a local queue of everything you do. Works captures ride the same outbox and the same sync button as map edits. A Works-enabled pack carries your open and work-ordered defects on the packed assets, your own non-terminal work orders, and every active template. It’s frozen at build time, and whether it includes Works at all is decided by your wam:read at that moment.

7.3–7.8 Build, go offline, sync

Build a pack for Femi, take it offline, work a round — start and complete orders, run an inspection, log a defect (including an orphan by GPS) — then sync. Drop the connection halfway and sync again: device-minted ids mean nothing doubles.

∑ Why sync is replay-safe

Every record created in the field gets a unique id on the device, before it syncs. When the server sees a create it already has, it returns the existing record instead of making a second. Inspections get extra care — recording has side effects (it raises defects), so the server checks “have I already recorded this inspection id?” first, and a replay doesn’t re-raise anything. Status updates and start/complete are naturally safe (last write wins).

Pitfalls
Build the pack close to departure. Check the manifest counts before you lose signal. Make titles and notes specific. Sync early, sync often, sync without fear. Log the defect — don’t wait to make the order. Photograph asset-anchored problems; describe orphan ones. Don’t hunt for other crews’ work.

Permissions, territory & the agent

Two honest sharp edges — and the whole module through MCP.

8.1–8.3 Two permissions, one method-aware gate

One gate at the module’s mount: GET requires wam:read, POST/PATCH requires wam:write. The browser UI, the field app and the agent all pass through it. Per role:

Because the assignee picker is exactly the set of wam:write holders, you can never assign work to someone who can’t complete it.

8.4 The first sharp edge: write is flat

There is no “template author” permission. Anyone who can write to Works can create — and retire — templates the entire organisation runs. The blast radius is bounded (templates can’t be deleted, only retired/restored; recorded inspections snapshot their items), so manage it by convention: agree who curates the library. If it must be locked down, that’s a permission-model change, not a setting. See it happen:

8.5 The second sharp edge: Works is not territory-scoped

The map is spatially access-controlled; Works is not. A user with wam:read sees the entire board, org-wide; with wam:write they can act on any record, wherever the asset sits. Territory reaches Works only indirectly — through the feature-panel Works tab (scoped by the asset you can open) and the field pack (area-filtered). The /works board and the agent tools go straight at the wam schema and are bounded by neither. Treat wam:read as an organisation-wide grant, and don’t use Works as a data-isolation boundary.
