01

The map & your tools

The workspace, getting around, the layers panel, and a tour of every everyday tool.

The map is where most of an operator’s day happens. This chapter is the one piece of orientation the rest of the book leans on: how to move around, how to control what’s drawn, and what each tool on the toolbar is for. After it, every recipe will name a tool and assume you know where it lives.

1.1 The workspace at a glance 🗺️

Open Map — it’s where VoltEdge lands you. Four things frame everything you’ll do. Hover (or tap) each numbered part:

  • The map canvas — your network drawn over a basemap, filling the screen.
  • The toolbar — the buttons and menus you act with (create, select, measure, snap…). §1.5 tours them.
  • Floating panels — detail that appears over the map (a feature’s panel, trace results, the query builder). They move and resize; §1.6.
  • The status bar — a quiet strip along the bottom with live readouts of where and how you’re looking; §1.4.

For the concepts behind the workspace — signing in, the data model, permissions — see the User Handbook. This book stays on the doing.

1.2 Getting around: pan, zoom, box-zoom

▶ Quick steps
  1. Pan — 🖱️ click-drag the map.
  2. Zoom — mouse wheel / trackpad, or the on-screen + / −.
  3. Jump to an area precisely — click Box-zoom on the toolbar, then 🖱️ drag a rectangle around the street or substation; the map fits exactly that box.
  4. Straighten the map — if you’ve rotated it off north, click Reset north (the compass button) to animate back to north-up.
Worked example · PriyaPriya needs to look at a specific pole-top on Feeder 7. She could wheel-zoom in stages, but instead she clicks Box-zoom and drags a small rectangle around the junction — one gesture and the map frames exactly that corner. Done inspecting, she notices she’d nudged the map’s rotation while panning; the compass button has appeared, so she clicks it and the view snaps back to north-up.

How it works. 🗺️ As you zoom, the map redraws, and some feature classes and labels only appear once you’re zoomed in far enough — an administrator sets those thresholds so a country-wide view isn’t buried in detail. 💡 If a class of asset “isn’t on the map,” the two first things to check are (a) are you zoomed in past its threshold, and (b) is its layer switched on (§1.3).

Where the map opens
The first time you open the map in a session it starts at your organisation’s configured default. After that, VoltEdge remembers your last centre and zoom as you move between pages — hop to the Features browser and back and you’re right where you left off (for the current session).

1.3 Controlling what’s drawn: the layers panel & basemaps

▶ Quick steps
  1. Open the Layers panel from the toolbar.
  2. Show/hide a feature class — toggle transformers, switches, conductors, … on or off to declutter.
  3. Bring in operational overlays — toggle Outages and Faults to lay live context over the network.
  4. Adjust an external layer — for any configured third-party layer, toggle it and drag its opacity so it blends rather than blots out your network.
  5. Switch the background — use the basemap picker (bottom of the map) for streets, a light reference style, or a plain/blank backdrop.
Worked example · DanaDana is about to add a transformer and wants a clean canvas. She opens the Layers panel and switches off half a dozen classes she doesn’t need, leaving only conductors, poles, and transformers visible. She flips the basemap from streets to the blank backdrop so nothing competes with her network — and because the blank basemap adapts to the theme, it stays dark in her dark-mode session rather than glaring white.

How it works. The layers panel is your control board for what is drawn. It’s the first place to look when an expected asset type is missing — it may simply be toggled off here, or hidden by the zoom thresholds from §1.2. Feature classes, operational overlays (OMS/Faults), and any external service layers each get their own section.

1.4 The status bar

Along the bottom sits a strip of live readouts: the cursor position (longitude/latitude under your pointer), a live zoom (Zoom: N.N, handy when you’re matching those class/label thresholds), the projection and the basemap. In the full status bar below you’ll also meet the API-health dot, box-zoom, measure, go-to-coordinate and pins (§10.6).

1.5.1 Select vs. a plain click 🖱️

This trips people up, so learn it now:

  • A plain single click on a feature opens its detail panel — this is how you inspect or edit one thing (Chapter 2).

1.6 Floating panels — they move and resize

Much of VoltEdge’s detail appears in floating panels over the map: the feature panel, trace and analysis results, the query builder, the assistant. They share behaviour worth knowing on day one:

  • They float over the map so you keep your geographic context.
  • Most are draggable (grab the header to reposition) and resizable (drag the grip), so you can arrange your workspace.
  • 💡 When a panel covers something you need to see, you don’t have to close it — just drag it aside. (There’s a draggable one to play with in §10.5.)

1.7 Light/dark mode, and picking up where you left off

  • Remembered view — as noted in §1.2, the map keeps your last centre and zoom for the session, so navigating away and back doesn’t lose your place.

Finding & opening things

Search, click-to-open, the Features browser, and select-vs-highlight.

Before you can edit, measure, or connect a thing, you have to get to it. VoltEdge gives you several routes to the same destination — an open feature — and the right one depends on how you know the asset: by where it is, by what class it is, by an attribute, by proximity, or by the tag stuck to its side. This chapter is a menu of those routes. Pick the one that matches what you already know.

2.1 Open one feature by clicking it 🖱️

▶ Quick steps
  1. Zoom to where the asset is (Box-zoom from §1.2 is quick).
  2. 🖱️ Single-click the feature.
  3. Its detail panel opens over the map — attributes, terminals, connections, relationships, history, tags.
  4. If several features are stacked under your click, a small disambiguation list appears instead — click the one you meant.
Worked example · PriyaPriya wants to read the nameplate of a transformer she can see on Feeder 7. She box-zooms to the pole, single-clicks the transformer symbol, and its panel slides in — she reads the rating without changing anything, because a plain click opens, it doesn’t edit. A few streets over she clicks again but the transformer sits right on top of a fuse and a pole; instead of guessing, VoltEdge shows her a short candidates list of the stacked features and she picks the transformer from it.

How it works. A plain click writes the hit into the selection store, which the feature panel reads and opens. When a single click lands on several overlapping features, VoltEdge deliberately does not guess — it shows the stacked candidates for you to disambiguate (the same picker connect-mode uses for choosing a target). A click that resolves to exactly one feature — including picking one out of the candidates list — goes straight to the open panel.

  • 💡 A single click is always safe: it only opens. Nothing is written until you deliberately edit (Chapter 4) or move geometry (Chapter 5).
  • ⚠️ Select ≠ click. The Select tool (§1.5.1) highlights features without opening any panel. If clicking doesn’t open a panel, check you’re not still in the Select tool — press Escape to leave it.
  • 🗺️ Only classes that have geometry and are switched on and past their zoom threshold are clickable on the map. Can’t click what you can’t see — revisit §1.2–§1.3, or use the browser (§2.2).
Try it
In Interactive 1 above, zoom in on the pole at the end of Feeder 7 (use Box-zoom) and click it. Then switch the pole layer off and click again — you can’t click what isn’t drawn.

2.2 Page a class in the Features browser

The Features browser (left-nav → Features) is the home for every compiled feature class, and the only place the geometry-less classes ever appear — protective relays, bays, substation panels, station batteries and the like never draw on the map, so this is where you read them.

▶ Quick steps
  1. Open Features from the sidebar.
  2. In the left list, filter by class — type in the “Search classes…” box (it matches the class name/code, not individual features).
  3. Click a class to load its rows on the right.
  4. Expand any row (the chevron) to read its full, schema-labelled attributes.
  5. For a row that has geometry, click Show on map (the pin icon) to jump to the map with that feature selected and centred.
Worked example · DanaDana needs to audit every protective relay in Oakwood, but relays have no map symbol — they’re pure records. She opens the Features browser, types “relay” to filter the class list down to PROTECTIVE_RELAY, and clicks it. The right pane pages through every relay; she expands three of them to compare their settings inline. Later she’s in the browser looking at a transformer row and wants to see where it is, so she clicks its Show on map pin — VoltEdge selects that transformer and drops her on the map centred right on it, even though it wasn’t in her last viewport.

2.3 Find features by an attribute — the Query builder (Filter mode)

When you know something about the features but not where they are — a rating, a status, a name fragment — build a query.

▶ Quick steps
  1. On the map, open the Query builder.
  2. Pick the feature class to query.
  3. In Filter mode, add one or more conditions — a field, an operator (equals, not equals, <, ≤, >, ≥, contains, starts with, …), and a value. Nest groups with AND/OR if you need to.
  4. Run the query.
  5. Click a result row’s zoom button to select that feature and fly the map to it; use Download to export the result set.
Worked example · TomTom is commissioning and needs every 315 kVA distribution transformer on the network. He opens the Query builder, chooses DIST_XFMR, adds the condition rated capacity equals 315, and runs it. A list comes back; he clicks the zoom button beside each row to hop the map straight to that transformer, verifying each in turn. When he’s done he hits Download to keep the list.

2.4 Find everything near a point — the Radius scope

“What’s within N metres of here?” is a spatial question, answered by giving a query a radius scope rather than a new panel.

▶ Quick steps
  1. Open the Query builder and go to Aggregate mode (or the All-classes breakdown).
  2. Set the scope to Radius of a point.
  3. 🖱️ Click a centre on the map and choose a radius (there are presets).
  4. Run — you get the count/breakdown of everything inside the circle, which stays drawn while the scope is active.
Worked example · PriyaAfter a car hits a pole, Priya needs to know what plant sits within 150 m of it. She opens the Query builder, switches to the all-classes breakdown, sets the scope to Radius of a point, clicks the pole as the centre and picks 150 m. VoltEdge draws the circle and returns a per-class tally of everything inside it — spans of conductor, a transformer, poles — so she can brief the crew before they roll.

How it works. The operators and nesting the panel offers mirror the backend query engine exactly — the UI never sends a condition or a nesting depth the server would reject (groups nest up to five deep). Every result row carries a zoom button that selects the feature with focus, so the map pans and zooms to it even if it wasn’t loaded — the same behaviour as the trace results table. Results respect your territory access, so you only ever get features you’re allowed to see. Radius scope shares the map’s radius-query store: selecting it arms the map so your next click sets the centre, and the geodesic circle is drawn only while that scope is active — leave the scope and the circle clears. The scope is sent to the same aggregate endpoint as a {kind: "radius", center, radius_m} object, so the count you see is computed server-side against real geometry, not eyeballed.

  • 💡 Switch the panel to Aggregate mode for counts instead of rows — count, count-by-field, or sum/avg/min/max of a numeric field, plus an all-classes breakdown of “what’s on the map within this scope.” Drill a count down to the rows behind it and highlight them on the map.
  • 💡 There’s a dedicated tag filter, too — “features that have a tag” or match a tag code/prefix. See the Asset Tags Guide, find-by-tag.
History

Radius querying used to be a standalone map tool; it now lives inside the Query builder as a scope, alongside territory scope (“everything under this part of the tree”). One panel, three scopes: none, territory, radius.

03

Creating features

Every geometry type, from a point transformer to a multi-vertex line.

Putting a new asset on the map is the editor’s bread and butter. Every creation follows the same two-step shape — draw the geometry, then fill the form — so once you’ve done one you’ve done them all; the only thing that changes is whether you’re placing a point, a line, or an area. This chapter walks each shape, the form that follows, and the small niceties (nudge before you commit, duplicate an existing asset) that make the day faster.

🔒 Needs features:write

Creating features needs features:write. Priya (viewer) doesn’t see the New feature button at all; Dana (editor) does. Everything in this chapter is Dana’s or Tom’s to do. 🗺️ You can only create within your territory — a save outside your access is refused. 3.0 The shape of every create

Two steps, always in this order:

  1. Draw the geometry — pick the class from New feature, then click the map to lay down its point, line, or area. This creates an unsaved draft.
  2. Fill the form & save — a create form opens in place of the feature panel: name (optional), subtype (if the class has them), and the class’s create-time fields. Save commits it; Cancel throws the draft away.

Nothing is written to the network until you press Save — right up to that moment a draft is yours alone, and you can even nudge its position (§3.5).

3.1 Create a point feature (transformer, pole, switch…)

▶ Quick steps
  1. Click New feature on the toolbar.
  2. Find the class — the list is grouped by category; type in Search classes… to filter by name or code (e.g. busbar, or MV_).
  3. Click the class. The banner reads “Click the map to place the new feature.”
  4. 🖱️ Click the spot on the map. The create form opens.
  5. Fill Name (optional), Subtype (if asked), and any required fields.
  6. Save.
Worked example · DanaDana is adding a new 315 kVA distribution transformer to a pole on Feeder 7. She clicks New feature, types “dist” to filter down to DIST_XFMR, and picks it. The banner tells her to click the map; she clicks the pole location and the create form appears. She types the name TX-Feeder7-015, sets the required primary-voltage field, and hits Save. The transformer appears on the map, its panel opens, and a moment later the assistant quietly suggests the connections it could now make (§3.8) — because she drew it right next to the pole and conductor.

How it works. Picking a class starts a draw session (the map owns the actual click-to-place). Finishing the placement turns it into a draft; the shared create form reads the class’s schema and shows only the fields marked visible on create. On Save it POSTs the name, subtype, geometry and those attributes to the create endpoint, then selects the new feature so its panel opens.

  • 💡 New features are born PLANNED — their lifecycle/operational_state defaults to Planned, so a freshly drawn asset doesn’t masquerade as energised (note the dashed symbol). Change it when you commission (Chapter 4).
  • ⌨️ Press Escape or click the banner’s ✕ to abandon a draw before you’ve placed anything.

3.2 Create a line feature (a conductor, a cable run)

A line is a sequence of clicks, finished with a double-click.

▶ Quick steps
  1. New feature → pick a line class (e.g. an overhead conductor). Banner: “Click to add points, double-click to finish the line.”
  2. 🖱️ Click each vertex along the route.
  3. 🖱️ Double-click the last point to finish.
  4. Fill the form and Save.
Worked example · TomTom is drawing an LV service run from the new transformer to a pillar. He picks the conductor class and starts clicking: the transformer end, a bend at the kerb, the pillar end — then double-clicks to close the line. Because he wants the run to actually connect, he’d flicked on Snap (Chapter 7) before starting, so his first click locked onto the transformer’s exact point rather than landing a metre off. The form opens; he sets the required fields and saves. The assistant immediately offers to wire the conductor to the transformer and the pillar it snapped to.

How it works. While a line (or polygon) draw is live, two extra controls appear beside Cancel: Snap (make the cursor stick to existing features — Chapter 7) and Ortho (constrain segments to 45° steps / straight runs). Each click adds a vertex; the double-click ends the geometry and drops you into the same create form as a point.

  • 💡 Snap is the difference between a line that connects and one that only looks connected. Turn it on before you place the first vertex when the ends should meet existing plant.
  • ⚠️ A misclick mid-line isn’t fatal — finish the line roughly, then reshape its vertices afterward (Chapter 5), or Cancel and redraw.

3.3 Create an area feature (a polygon)

Same as a line, but the shape closes into an area.

▶ Quick steps
  1. New feature → pick a polygon class. Banner: “Click to add points, double-click to finish the polygon.”
  2. 🖱️ Click each corner of the boundary.
  3. 🖱️ Double-click to close and finish.
  4. Fill the form and Save.
Worked example · DanaDana outlines a substation compound. She picks the polygon class, clicks the four corners of the fence line, and double-clicks to close it. The area fills in, the form opens, she names it and saves. Ortho helped her keep the fence corners square as she went.

How it works. Identical to a line — vertices by click, finish by double-click — except the geometry is closed into a ring. Snap and Ortho are available in the banner just as they are for lines.

3.4 Filling the create form

The form is the same regardless of shape. Read this once; it applies to every recipe above.

  • Name — optional, a human-readable label. Leave it blank and the feature still saves.
  • Subtype — if the class has subtypes, this is required (marked with a red *); the picker opens on “Select…”. The subtype you choose decides which attribute fields become required.
  • Attribute fields — only the class’s create-time fields show here (not every field the class owns). A field marked * is required for the chosen subtype, so the required set can change the moment you switch subtype.
  • Save stays disabled until every required piece is present — a missing subtype or a blank required field keeps it greyed out. (The form tells you exactly what’s still missing.)
Worked example · DanaDana starts an MV_CONDUCTOR and the form shows a subtype picker. The moment she chooses Overhead, three fields sprout red asterisks (conductor size, construction, span) that weren’t required a second earlier; had she picked Underground the required set would differ. She fills them, Save lights up, and she commits. (Try it in the workbench: draw a conductor, switch the subtype back and forth.)
  • 💡 If Save won’t enable, scan for a red * you haven’t filled — most often the Subtype itself.
  • ⚠️ The form only offers create-time fields by design; other attributes are set later from the feature panel (Chapter 4). You’re not missing anything — e.g. a transformer’s serial number isn’t on the create form.

3.5 Nudge a draft before you commit 🖱️

You don’t have to place a point perfectly on the first click.

▶ Quick steps
  1. After placing a point/line/polygon (form open, not yet saved)…
  2. 🖱️ Drag the draft’s geometry on the map to reposition it.
  3. Your typed form values stay put through the drag.
  4. Save when it’s where you want it.
Worked example · DanaDana drops the transformer, then notices it’s sitting a little off the pole. Rather than cancel and start over, she drags the draft marker onto the pole; the name and fields she’d already typed are untouched. Satisfied, she saves.

How it works. A draft’s geometry can be moved right up until Save, and the form deliberately preserves what you’ve entered while you drag — only a genuinely new draft resets the form. (With Snap on, the dragged draft’s anchor will lock onto a nearby feature — the amber ring.)

3.6 Duplicate an existing feature

The fastest way to make “another one like that.”

▶ Quick steps
  1. Open the feature you want to copy (single-click it — Chapter 2).
  2. In its panel, choose Duplicate.
  3. A draft opens pre-filled with the original’s name, subtype and attributes.
  4. Place it (drag to position), adjust anything, and Save.
Worked example · DanaDana needs three more identical LV pillars along a street. She opens the first, hits Duplicate, and a new draft appears already carrying its subtype and attributes — she just drags it to the next location and saves, twice more. Three consistent pillars in a fraction of the time.

How it works. Duplicate seeds a fresh draft from the source feature’s values, then runs it through the same create form and save path as a blank draw — so validation, the visible-on-create filter, and the post-save select all behave identically. Copied attributes the create validator wouldn’t accept (computed/invisible ones) are dropped automatically.

3.7 What you can’t create from the map here

Two honest exclusions:

  • Geometry-less classes (relays, bays, panels, station batteries) have no map shape, so they’re not in the New feature list (open the menu above — there’s no relay). They come into being another way — as part of a composite (a switchgear panel fans out its devices), or by digitising onto a diagram. See the substation and feature-catalog guides.

4.5 When someone edited it first — the concurrency guard

Worked example · DanaDana opens a transformer and starts editing. Meanwhile a colleague saves a change to the same transformer. (Reproduce it: open a feature, change something, press “Tom saves the same feature meanwhile”, then Save — twice.)

A point feature has neither a length nor an area, so its panel simply doesn’t show those rows. 6.6 How measurement is computed (and why it’s trustworthy)

  
Both the Measure tool and the panel measure geodesically — on the curved earth, not on the flat web-map projection. VoltEdge measures the real geometry with a haversine-based engine (getLength / getArea), so the numbers stay correct regardless of latitude. This matters: a naive measurement on a Web-Mercator map exaggerates distances the further you are from the equator — a 1 km span near the poles can look like several. VoltEdge doesn’t have that error; a kilometre is a kilometre whether you’re in Accra or Reykjavík.

You can’t always see the gap at map zoom, but a trace can: an almost-connection is an open circuit. Connecting & relating
Terminals, connections, relationships, and guided assemblies.

  
This is the most technical chapter in the book, and the most important, so it goes slowly and explains why before how. Everything up to now has been about individual features — their shape, place, and attributes. But VoltEdge isn’t a drawing; it’s a network model, and a model is only as good as the links between its parts. A transformer that looks joined to a conductor but isn’t will never appear in a trace, never carry load flow, never de-energise in an outage. This chapter is how you make the joins real.

8.1 Where connectivity lives — and which features can have it

  
Open a feature and look at its tabs. The Terminals tab (the plug icon) is where electrical connections live — but it only appears for features that are part of the network model. In VoltEdge terms, a feature has a network role, and only two roles can carry connectivity:

  
    
    
  • An edge (a wire — MV_CONDUCTOR, a cable) — it connects two junctions but has no terminals of its own.
    • 
    
  • A junction (a device or node — a transformer, switch, busbar) — it carries the terminals that edges plug into.
    • 
  

  
Everything else — a container (a substation boundary, a bay) or a geometry-less class (a relay) — has no place in the current path, so it has no Terminals tab at all. If you can’t find the tab, that’s why: the feature isn’t a connectable network element. (The trace button is hidden for the same reason — a non-network feature never appears in a trace.)

  
  • 💡 This is also why the terminal dropdowns you’ll meet below sometimes show nothing: an edge like a conductor has no terminals to pick, so its side of a connection is just “wire it in”, while a junction like a switch offers its T1 (source) / T2 (load) terminals to choose from.

Personalising & getting unstuck
Settings that make the map yours, and a field guide for when it doesn’t behave.

  
Nine chapters in, you can find things, create them, edit their attributes and geometry, measure, snap, connect, relate and annotate. This closing chapter is about the space around all of that: first, the handful of settings that make the map feel like yours — theme, basemap, units, which layers you see, where your panels sit — and then a troubleshooting guide for the moments the app doesn’t do what you expect, so you’re never stuck wondering whether it’s you or it.

  
    
    
  • Part A — Making it yours (§10.1–§10.6). Every personal setting, what it changes, and — the part people get wrong — whether it survives a reload.     2. 
    
  2. Pick Light, Dark, or System. A ✓ marks the active choice.
    3. 
    
  3. The whole app switches instantly. Your choice is remembered across reloads.
    4. 
  

  
Worked example · Priya moves to the night shiftPriya is picking up evening dispatch and the bright white map is tiring in a dim control room. She clicks the sun icon in the header and chooses Dark. The map chrome, panels and status bar all flip to the dark surface at once; the map tiles stay the same, but the blank basemap (if she’d chosen it) now shows a near-black background rather than glaring white (that specific fix is why the blank basemap tracks the theme — see §10.11). ▶ Quick steps

    2. 
    
  2. Everything re-labels at once — metres/kilometres and hectares/km² become feet/miles and acres, or the reverse.
    3. 
    
  3. Your choice is remembered across reloads.
    4. 
  

  
Worked example · Dana quotes a span in the customer’s unitsDana is measuring a proposed overhead extension for a customer who thinks in feet. She’s been working in metric all morning. This is the single most common confusion, and it has exactly three causes. Work down them in order:

  
    
    
  1. You don’t hold the permission. VoltEdge hides an action you can’t perform rather than showing it disabled — so a missing Edit pencil, New feature menu, Annotate menu, Manage files button, Add link, or the connect/relate buttons usually means you lack the permission behind it. The pattern throughout this book: creating and editing features need features:write; drawing redlines needs annotations:write; managing files needs attachments:write; live-data links need metadata:read. Priya, a pure viewer, simply doesn’t see any of these — and that’s the design, not a fault.
    2. 
    
  2. It’s outside your territory. Even with the right permission, write actions are gated to your territory access (🗺️). A feature that belongs to a zone you can’t edit will open read-only. If you can see something but not change it, territory is the usual reason.
    3. 
    
  3. Your build predates it. If a button described here is missing for everyone, including an administrator, your VoltEdge deployment may be older than the feature. That’s the honest third possibility, and it’s the one to raise with whoever runs your instance.
    4.

10.8 “My edit was rejected — someone changed it first”

  
Occasionally you save an edit and instead of success you’re told the feature changed since you opened it. That’s not a lost edit — it’s a concurrency guard doing its job.

  
What’s happening. When you start editing, the app notes the feature’s state at that moment. On save it checks that nobody else has modified the feature in the meantime. If someone has — say Tom updated the same switch from the field while Dana had it open — the save is refused so the second writer can’t silently clobber the first.

  
The fix. Your in-progress edit isn’t discarded — your typed values stay in the form. Close and reopen the feature (or otherwise refresh it) so you’re looking at the current, saved state, then re-apply your change on top of it and save again. Now you’re editing from the latest version and the save goes through. (You can reproduce it in Interactive 8 — Chapter 4.)

  
    
    
  • 💡 This only fires on a genuine collision — two people (or a person and a field sync) touching the same feature in an overlapping window. It’s rare, and it’s protecting you.
    • 
    
  • ⚠️ Don’t try to force it by hammering Save — reopen the feature so you can see what the other person changed before you decide how to merge your edit with it.
    • 
  


  
10.9 “This feature has no Terminals tab”

  
You open a feature to wire it up and there’s no Terminals tab at all. This isn’t a permission problem and it isn’t a bug.

  
Why. The Terminals tab — and the connectivity workflow behind it (§8.1–§8.2) — only appears for features that play an electrical network role of edge (a line the trace walks along, like a conductor) or junction (a point the trace walks through, like a switch). Features that are containers (a substation boundary, a bay) or have no geometry don’t carry terminals, so the tab is correctly absent. It’s the same rule that decides whether the Trace button shows. (Click the substation compound or a pole in Interactive 15 and read the panel.)

  
What to do instead. If two such features genuinely belong together, you almost certainly want a relationship (§8.6), not a connection — a structural “part of / associated with” link that doesn’t need terminals. Open the Relationships tab (which is there) and relate them.

  
  • 💡 Rule of thumb: if the trace should flow through it, it’s a network feature and has Terminals; if it merely contains or describes other things, it’s related, not connected.


  
10.10 “My connection didn’t take — the vertices won’t land”

  
A cable you drew looks connected to a switch but a trace stops dead there, or the plausibility check (§8.6) grumbles that two things you meant to join aren’t actually touching. The usual culprit is snapping.

  
Why. Two features are electrically joined only when you make the join explicitly (§8.2) — but even a geometric coincidence relies on the endpoints being exactly on top of each other, and by eye you’ll always be a pixel or two off. Snap (Chapter 7) is what pulls your vertex precisely onto the existing feature so there’s no invisible gap. Snap defaults to on, but it’s session-only — if you (or a habit) turned it off earlier, it stays off for the rest of that session, and every vertex you place after that is wherever your cursor happened to be.

  
The fix.

  
    
    
  1. Check the Snap (magnet) toggle — in the draw/edit banner, or in the Measure controls in the status bar. If it’s off, turn it back on.
    2. 
    
  2. Re-place the offending endpoint so it snaps onto the target feature (§7.1). You’ll feel it catch.
    3. 
    
  3. Make sure you actually created the connection (§8.2) — snapping the geometry together is necessary but a trace follows explicit connectivity, not mere overlap.
    4. 
  

  
    
    
  • ⚠️ Snap off is the classic source of “fake” connections — geometry that looks joined at full zoom but has a hair-fine gap. If a trace ends one feature too early, suspect this first.
    • 
    
  • 💡 The assistant’s suggestions (§8.3) and the loop check (§8.2) are your safety nets here: use them to confirm the join is real rather than trusting the picture. (Interactive 15 draws a red ring wherever geometry looks joined but isn’t.)
    • 
  


  
10.11 “The map looks wrong”

  
A grab-bag of visual “that’s not right” moments, each with a quick cause:

  
    
    
  • Everything’s tilted. The view has been rotated off north (a two-finger twist is easy to trigger by accident). Click the reset-north arrow at the top-left (§10.6) to spin back to north-up. It only appears because you’re rotated.
    • 
    
  • The background went solid, not blank-white. In dark mode, the Blank basemap shows a near-black background rather than white — that’s intentional, so a no-tiles map doesn’t glare white in a dark room. It’s not a failed load; it’s the blank basemap tracking your theme (§10.1–§10.2).
    • 
    
  • My view jumped back to the default. You did a full page reload. The remembered centre/zoom (§10.6) survives navigating between pages but not a hard reload — after a reload the map opens at the administrator’s default extent by design. Just pan/zoom back, or use a pin (§10.6) if it’s somewhere you return to.
    • 
    
  • A whole class vanished. Check the Layers panel (§10.4) — you (or a saved preference from last session) may have that class hidden. Its checkbox will be clear; set it to bring the class back. Remember hiding is persisted, so a class you hid last week is still hidden today.
    • 
    
  • Nothing is loading at all. Glance at the API health dot at the far left of the status bar. If it’s red, the app can’t reach the server — that’s an infrastructure issue to raise, not something a map setting will fix. (Tick “API down” in Interactive 1.)
