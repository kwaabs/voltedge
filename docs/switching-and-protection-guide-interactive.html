Switchgear and protection are two halves of one story: a device that can interrupt a feeder, and a setting that decides when it does. This guide is a working copy of the real rules — JavaScript ports of the trace engine (barriers, the de-energised cone, isolate, sections and zones, the layer guard), the log-log TCC interpolation, the coordination grader, the fault and arc-flash maths and the restoration study — all run on one small network, Feeder 7, in your browser. Open a switch, tag a cable out, drag a time dial, break a curve, and watch the same answers the server would give.

Use As in the top bar. Dana and Priya are editors (features:write, connectivity:commit, trace:run, oms:write …) — there is no “dispatcher” role, so the dispatcher is an editor. Tom is an analyst (reads and runs traces/studies, cannot change a switch). Ada is an admin (adds metadata:write). Sam is a viewer (read-only). Widgets that change the model say what they need and lock politely when your persona lacks it.

Who this is for

Network modellers capturing switchgear and protection; control engineers who operate the model (open, close, isolate, restore); and protection engineers who set relays and check coordination. The full manual (switching-and-protection-guide.md) holds every table, SQL recipe and the corrections list; this edition keeps all of its sections and makes each idea something you can poke.

Three honest limits up front, because they decide how far to trust a screen: the “dead” you see is “downstream of an open device”, not “unsupplied” (closing a tie does not clear it); nothing simulates a recloser’s shots or a sectionalizer’s count — the model stores the settings and you record the outcome in device_state; and the grader compares one operate time per device at one fault current. Chapter 11 lists what the model does not do.

02

The switching devices

Eight inline classes, one container, one legacy class — and three things that are fields, not classes.

Every switching device is a point feature with network_role = 'junction' that sits inline on a conductor and can interrupt it. They share one shape: two terminals (T1 primary = line side, T2 secondary = load side), three connectivity rules that let a trace walk through a closed device and stop at an open one, and two always-required fields — device_state (the live position) and condition_rating (GOOD/FAIR/POOR). The MV and LV switching classes are geometry_optional and diagram-placeable, so a switch can live on the map, on a one-line, or both; the tie assembly is the exception and needs map geometry.

Three things are fields or assemblies, not classes. A bus coupler is an MV_BREAKER with switch_function = BUS_COUPLER, wired bus-to-bus. A feeder tie / normal-open point is an MV_SWITCH with normal_state = NORMALLY_OPEN, wired conductor-to-conductor. An RMU way is a plain MV_SWITCH contained by the RMU; ring vs transformer way is is_fused. There is no CIRCUIT_BREAKER, DISCONNECTOR, LOAD_BREAK, RMU_WAY or RMU_BUSBAR class.

03

The two state axes (plus normal state)

Confusing device_state with operational_state is the number-one modelling mistake.

A switching device carries two independent state columns. device_state (CLOSED/OPEN) is the live position — the only field that reconfigures the network by itself. operational_state (PLANNED → IN_SERVICE → ISOLATED → RETIRED) is the asset lifecycle; ISOLATED and RETIRED also block a trace (the asset isn’t really there). A closed switch that is ISOLATED still blocks flow — the asset is unavailable, not switched open. Two more descriptive fields: normal_state (NORMALLY_CLOSED/NORMALLY_OPEN, the intended position; a label that does not itself block flow) and, on breakers, switch_function (FEEDER, INCOMER, BUS_COUPLER, BUS_SECTION, TRANSFORMER).

Interactive · 4

Set the axes — barrier, legal jumps and the normal-vs-live reading

∑ Engineer

operational_state is a state machine. Illegal jumps are refused with a 400 at write time (crud.UpdateFeature → metadata.domain_transitions). Legal edges: PLANNED→IN_SERVICE, PLANNED→RETIRED, IN_SERVICE→ISOLATED, IN_SERVICE→RETIRED, ISOLATED→IN_SERVICE, ISOLATED→RETIRED; NULL→any and any→NULL always. Nothing leaves RETIRED. Existing rows are back-filled: published → IN_SERVICE, draft → PLANNED. The field exists on conductors, busbars, the MV/LV switching classes, DIST_XFMR, SERVICE_POINT and others — not on POWER_XFMR, so a power transformer cannot be tagged out this way.

normal_state exists on MV_BREAKER, MV_ISOLATOR, MV_SWITCH, MV_SECTIONALIZER and MV_RECLOSER — not on MV_FUSE or LV_SWITCH. Three things read it: the diagram’s amber NOP marker (only for NORMALLY_OPEN), the tie assembly (sets it), and the contingency study, which ranks NORMALLY_OPEN candidate ties first. It is nullable with no subtype rule.

The model does not alarm on the abnormal rows — nothing pages — but it now reports them. The Abnormal tab on the Operations page (Operations → Abnormal, operations:read) lists exactly the two abnormal rows above: every switching device whose live device_state contradicts its declared normal_state, with its current state, last operation and when. A standing report, not a notification — open it to answer “what is off-normal right now?” (after a storm, before handing back a feeder, during an audit); it won’t fire the moment a device goes off-normal. Behind it: deviceops.Abnormal discovers every class carrying both a device_state and a normal_state field and scans for the contradiction (api/internal/deviceops/abnormal.go:31-64), served at GET /operations/abnormal (deviceops/http.go:35) — no new table, it reads the live rows.

Who sets state — and OMS switching orders

Both columns are ordinary attributes (REST PATCH /features/…, the attribute editor, or the update_feature MCP tool). The OMS module flips device_state as crew execute a switching order: executing an open step sets OPEN, a close step sets CLOSED, un-ticking restores the opposite — inside the same transaction that ticks the step, through crud.UpdateFeature (validated, history-logged). A generated order always has five steps: isolate — open the blamed device, verify the downstream supplies are de-energised, earth at the nearest upstream isolation point; restore — note “remove earths”, close the device. Only open/close move a device. Status: draft → issued → in_progress → completed (or cancelled). Chapter 10 runs one.

Planned switching — a program with no incident

The order above is generated from an incident. For planned work — a maintenance isolation, a construction cut-over — author a standalone switching program tied to no incident. It reuses the very same oms.switching_orders / oms.switching_steps tables (migration 0186 made incident_id nullable and added a booked window + purpose), so it executes, logs and interlocks identically — the only difference is incident_id IS NULL. A program lives on Operations → Switching (route /switching): read with oms:read, build with oms:write. Start it empty and write each step, or seed it with the standard open → verify → earth → close steps for a chosen device (the same buildIsolateRestoreDrafts the incident generator uses), then add / edit / delete / reorder steps.

04

What opening a device does to the network

A barrier, a de-energised cone, an isolation set, a section — and a layer guard that keeps them honest.

A switch isn’t “wired into” a frozen graph — its state is read live at every hop of every trace. One device opening instantly changes four things. This chapter runs the real rules on Feeder 7: the widgets below call JavaScript ports of trace/graph.go (bfs), energization.go, isolate.go and section.go, using the real trace_tier, boundary_role and network_role of each class.

4.1 It becomes a trace barrier

Barrier rules say “on class X, if field f equals value v, halt here”. For switching that is device_state = OPEN on any class that has the field (every switch, breaker, isolator, recloser, sectionalizer, fuse) or operational_state IN (ISOLATED, RETIRED) — the latter attached to DOWNSTREAM_TRACE and UPSTREAM_TRACE only, but reaching conductors, busbars and transformers too, so a tagged-out cable is a barrier. A class with several rules halts on any. The barrier node is visited and reported but never expanded; the check runs on the start node too (which is why isolate and the cone start from a device’s neighbours). Barriers are only consulted when the configuration has stop_at_open_devices = true:

Trace configurationDirectionStops at open devices?
CONNECTED_TRACEconnectedno — the whole galvanic island
DOWNSTREAM_TRACE / UPSTREAM_TRACEdownstream / upstreamyes (and at ISOLATED/RETIRED)
SHORTEST_PATH_TRACEshortest pathno
LOOP_CHECK_TRACEshortest pathyes
MV_CIRCUIT_TRACEconnected, scope_to_circuitno — the boundary is circuit membership, not switch state
TRACE_TO_TRANSFORMER / LV_NETWORK_TRACEupstream / downstream, target DIST_XFMRyes

4.2 It de-energises what’s below it — the cone

The cone of an open device is everything a live, barrier-respecting downstream walk reaches from the device’s load-side neighbours. The network-wide dead set is the union of the cones of every open (or isolated/retired) device — that union is what the one-line’s Live shading paints.

Conservative — and over-eager at every NOP. The cone starts at the open device and goes down; it never asks whether something else is feeding that region. So a region back-fed through a closed tie is still “dead”, and the far side of a normally-open tie is in the open tie’s cone — the conductor and loads just beyond T-79 are shaded in the Live view even though Feeder 9’s breaker is feeding them. Read “dead” as “downstream of an open device”, not “unsupplied”.

4.3 Transformers, busbars and the layer guard

  • Transformer crossing. The interactive map trace stops at a transformer-role feature unless the result table’s “Continue past transformers” is ticked. Every other consumer — energisation, isolate, feeder, service, section — passes through transformers into the LV side, because stopping would strand a transformer’s whole LV network as de-energised. An isolate on FSW-12 lists TX-220 and SP-220.
  • Junction pass-through. Any network_role = 'junction' feature is expanded undirected on directional traces, and everything discovered from it is “unlocked” and expands undirected too. That includes busbars and RMU buses and every switching device, transformer and service point, because they are all junction in the seeds (only conductors are edge). In a plain radial feeder it changes nothing; at a bus whose ways are all authored “switch → bus” it is what lets the walk reach the sibling ways; at a closed tie it carries the walk into the other feeder. Starting a downstream trace at a bus-attached breaker therefore reaches the sibling feeders on that bus.
  • Cross-layer guard (trace_tier). A downstream step may not jump to a strictly-lower tier, nor an upstream step to a strictly-higher one — it stops the undirected expansion leaking back toward the source (an 11 kV conductor → POWER_XFMR at tier 200 is refused). Untiered classes (legacy DIST_SWITCH) always pass. Blocked neighbours are not marked visited, so a legitimate deeper edge can still reach them.

4.4 “What if I open this?” — the isolate trace

POST /trace/isolate answers the switching question without mutating live state. It needs a DOWNSTREAM trace configuration and a device (a junction — the menu item is hidden otherwise). It returns: (1) the de-energised set — a downstream walk from the device’s downstream neighbours (other open devices in it are barriers: listed, but they cut the walk); (2) the upstream isolation boundary — an upstream walk to the nearest controller of boundary_role protective or switching; (3) critical facilities in the dead set (load_priority rank P1–P2) and possible back-feed paths (any connected neighbour of the dead set not already in the result); (4) a service rollup (customers, kW, by tier).

Read the boundary, don’t trust it blindly. The upstream walk starts at the device, and a switching device is a junction, so the walk begins undirected: devices at the same depth on the load side (for FSW-12, the RMU ring-in way beside it) can be hits alongside the true upstream device. The API then returns only the first hit of an unordered set. Confirm the boundary against the one-line before you tag anything. (The widget below lists all hits and marks which are really upstream.) Also: a normally-open tie’s own switch is in the dead list (a visited barrier); the back-feed entry is the first node beyond it.

4.5 Sections vs. zones

POST /trace/section bounds a region by walking out until it hits — but does not cross — a boundary device, and the kind of boundary distinguishes two ideas:

RegionBounded by (boundary_role)Means
Switching sectionprotective + switching + transformer + source“the smallest piece I can isolate to work on it” — a plain MV_SWITCH does bound it
Protection zoneprotective + transformer + source“what de-energises when a fault here trips the protection” — a plain switch does not bound it
Isolation boundary (isolate’s upstream leg)protective + switchingthe devices you would open — a transformer isn’t openable

The boundary devices are part of the result (visited, then not crossed). On Feeder 7: the switching section around C-2 runs from REC-7 to FSW-12 and the ring-in way; the protection zone around C-2 runs from REC-7 all the way down to the two distribution transformers — FSW-12, Way A and the other switches do not bound it. 05

Ties, bus couplers and RMUs

Three guided assemblies that create the device and wire it in one transaction.

These three aren’t built by hand — each is a composite on the map’s Assemblies menu (top-right, next to New feature) that creates the device and its connections inside one transaction, so a failure anywhere leaves nothing half-wired. Permissions: tie and coupler 🔒 features:write + connectivity:commit; RMU 🔒 features:write + connectivity:commit + relationships:commit (it also writes the containment relationships).

Feeder tie (NOP) POST /ties

Pick the switch class (default MV Switch; the list offers every active class that has a normal_state with NORMALLY_OPEN and both conductor rules). Map geometry is required. Two feeder pickers (feeder_a, feeder_b, different features). Born NORMALLY_OPEN and OPEN; an explicit value you supply wins. Wired feeder A → T1 and T2 → feeder B: A is the supply side.

Bus coupler POST /couplers

Pick the breaker class (MV Breaker); two bus pickers (bus_a, bus_b, different). Wired bus A.T2 → breaker.T1 and breaker.T2 → bus B.T1; stamped switch_function = BUS_COUPLER (you may pass BUS_SECTION). Downstream there is no coupler code — it is just a breaker.

Ring main unit POST /rmus

Pick rmu_config: 2W_1T (2 ring + 1 transformer → 3 ways) or 2W_2T (2 ring + 2 transformer → 4 ways). Creates the RMU container, its internal MV_BUSBAR, the MV_SWITCH ways (each way’s T2 wired to the bus) and an underground cable MV_CONDUCTOR on each way’s T1. Fused (transformer) ways are stamped is_fused = true.

RMU: no rmu_config, a config with no template, or no geometry. Rule misses surface as the connectivity error with the wiring step named (“wiring feeder_a … → MV_SWITCH.T1”).

An RMU ring left closed is a mesh. The two ring ways both land on the RMU bus, so a ring closed at both ends is a loop: traces cope (they carry on past visited nodes) but the power-flow engines refuse a non-radial network (“loop/mesh detected … run LOOP_CHECK_TRACE first”). Keep a normally-open point in every ring.

Modelling constraint. A real coupler bay often has flanking isolators, but no switch-to-switch network rule exists, so only the breaker is modelled; likewise a tie is a single switch.

06

The protection devices

Switching devices carry current; protection devices decide when to interrupt it.

The protective intelligence (the relay and its curves) is modelled separately from the thing it opens (the breaker), and a grading engine checks that they trip in the right order.

The relay — PROTECTIVE_RELAY

A diagram-only feature (geometry_type = 'none', network_role = 'none'): on a single-line diagram, never on the map, never in a trace or load flow. One subtype (standard); the functional variant is its ANSI device_number (27, 59, 50_51, 50_51N, 86, 87, 25, 32, 37, 46, 49, 64, 67, 67N, 79, 81). Only the overcurrent settings are graded — curve_type, pickup_current_a (primary amps), time_dial (unset or ≤ 0 is graded as 1), inst_pickup_a (reached → 0 s) and definite_time_s (for DEFINITE_TIME; ignores the dial). The engine never reads device_number itself: a relay linked by protection_trip is graded from whatever overcurrent settings it carries, whatever its ANSI code — so a 27 or 87 relay with a curve filled in would be graded as an overcurrent relay, and one with no settings is stored but never simulated.

A relay isn’t wired with connectivity rules — it uses two relationship classes: protection_trip (“this relay operates that device”) — targets MV_BREAKER and MV_RECLOSER, the legacy DIST_SWITCH and, after the DIST_SWITCH migration seed, MV_SWITCH; ⚠️ only a breaker’s relay is ever read, and with several relays on one breaker the engine grades the first (oldest link) and says so. protection_sensing — fed by an MV_CT/MV_VT: documentation; nothing in the Go code reads it.

MV_FUSE — switching and protection

An inline point junction. device_state = OPEN makes it a barrier like any switch, but it also grades as a protective device: subtypes expulsion_fuse, current_limiting_fuse; fuse_rating_a and fuse_speed (K, T, SLOW, FAST) are labels; curve_ref — the protection_curves.code of an ampere-quantity curve (free text, so any loaded manufacturer curve resolves; blank or unknown leaves the time unresolved) — drives the grading. The only fuse curve shipped is FUSE_100T_EXAMPLE, “NOT a real manufacturer sheet”.

MV_RECLOSER — protection as a device

A two-curve scheme: a fast_curve_ref (clears transient faults before a fuse blows) and a slow_curve_ref (delayed, coordinates with downstream fuses on a persistent fault), and after reclose_attempts (seed example: 4 operations) it goes to lockout, modelled as device_state = OPEN. In coordination it grades off its slow curve (falling back to the fast one).

Both curve fields name an ampere-quantity curve — not a relay curve, and there is no time dial. Typing IEC_VI there finds no total_clear points and the recloser silently drops out of the grading. No recloser curve ships — load one by SQL (Chapter 7). And the sequence (shots, dead time reclose_interval_s, reset reset_time_s, lockout) is documentation: no engine counts shots. Chapter 8 reasons about a sequence step by step.

Fused switches, sectionalizers

An MV_SWITCH/LV_SWITCH with is_fused = true is graded exactly like an MV_FUSE via the same fuse_rating_a/curve_ref — that is how an RMU transformer way protects its transformer. An unfused switch on the path is a plain topological hop (“switch is not fused — no protection element to grade”). A MV_SECTIONALIZER counts the fault passages a recloser’s trips cause and opens on the dead line after count_to_lockout (integer, nullable); it has no TCC so it is never time-graded, and OMS does not treat it as protective either.

Time-current curves

Tabulated points, log-log interpolation, and the three places the engine clamps.

A TCC curve is the promise “at this multiple of pickup, I operate in this many seconds.” VoltEdge stores curves as tabulated points, not live formulas (metadata.protection_curves + protection_curve_points, migration 0076). A curve has a unique code, a name, a standard and a quantity: multiple (relay curves — x-axis is a multiple of pickup, time at time_dial = 1) or ampere (fuse and recloser curves — x-axis is absolute amps). Its points are (band, current, time_s) with band = operate (relay) or minimum_melt / total_clear (fuse); the table enforces current > 0, time_s ≥ 0 and UNIQUE (curve_id, band, current).

7.1 The seeded catalogue

7.2 How the engine reads a curve

The engine never re-evaluates the formula: it log-log interpolates the stored points and then scales — t = time_dial × interp(I / pickup) for a relay, t = interp_total_clear(I) for a fuse, recloser or fused switch. 08

Coordination

“For a fault here, do the devices on the path trip in the right order, with enough margin?”

POST /analysis/coordination (🔒 analysis:run) works like this (∑):

  1. It reuses the fault substrate: builds the network tree from the start feature (stopping at open devices), resolves impedances and computes the prospective three-phase fault current at the fault node: I = (V_base / √3) / |Z| on a line-to-line nominal (voltage_basis: line_to_neutral drops the √3). The start’s own source_r_ohm/source_x_ohm seed the impedance — so a study started at a breaker has no source impedance and over-states the fault level.
  2. It refers that current to each device’s own voltage base across any transformer on the path: I_device = I_fault × V_fault / V_device.
  3. It walks source → fault and reads each time-graded device off its curve: a breaker via its PROTECTIVE_RELAY (instantaneous element first, then the curve × time_dial); a fuse via curve_ref total_clear; a recloser via slow_curve_ref (else fast_curve_ref); a fused switch as a fuse.
  4. The first device with a resolved time nearest the fault is the primary; every resolved device upstream is a backup. For each adjacent pair: margin = t_backup − t_primary, coordinated when margin ≥ CTI (default 0.3 s, overridable with cti_seconds; the map panel pre-fills 0.3).

Covered classes: MV_BREAKER, MV_FUSE, MV_SWITCH, LV_SWITCH, MV_RECLOSER. MV_SECTIONALIZER is explicitly not time-graded. A device with no resolved time (no relay link, missing pickup/curve, at or below pickup, unfused switch, no curve points) stays in the list with a note and is skipped, so a non-operating device between two graders doesn’t break the chain. The result carries the fault current, a Devices[] list (role, through-current, time, curve plot, extrapolated), a Pairs[] list and an overall verdict — absent when fewer than two devices could be graded. The fault must be reached by a live path downstream of the start; a fault behind an open device is refused (“fault feature is not downstream of start …”).

8.3 Reading a TCC plot, and what coordination does not check

Each device is one trace on a shared log-log chart (current on the common voltage base on x); a vertical line marks the fault current and each curve crosses it at that device’s operate time. A relay draws one trace (operate curve × TD, truncated at any instantaneous pickup); a fuse draws two (minimum melt and total clear). Coordination is a vertical gap at the fault line: the upstream curve must sit at least CTI above the downstream one — and, for a fuse–recloser pair, across the whole current range where both can see the fault, not only at the one fault current you studied.

The grading compares one operate time per device at one fault current. It does not model pre-load or cold-load pickup, fuse pre-damage from earlier shots, relay reset and overshoot, breaker opening time, ground-fault (50_51N) settings, directional/differential elements, CT saturation, sequence coordination, or fault-current decay. (The recloser’s fast curve is now checked — by the separate fuse-saving study, §8.5, not by the margin grader.)

8.4 Reclose sequences, sectionalizer counting, fuse-saving vs fuse-blowing

A recloser (reclose_attempts = 4) with the fast curve first — fast, fast, slow, slow — is the classic fuse-saving program. A transient fault clears on the first fast trip and the recloser closes after the dead time. A persistent fault steps through the operations and then locks out — device_state = OPEN — until a crew closes it. In the model the only record of the outcome is device_state: nothing counts the shots. A sectionalizer (SEC-4, count_to_lockout = 3) counts the recloser’s trips for faults beyond it and opens in the dead time after the count; the count must be less than the recloser’s operations. Fuse-saving needs fast total-clear × K < fuse minimum-melt at that current (K ≈ 1.25 is a rule of thumb for earlier-shot heating — not a VoltEdge setting); fuse-blowing needs the slow curve slower than the fuse’s total-clear. The margin grader (§8.1) covers only the slow-vs-total-clear pair at one current; the fast curve and the fuse-saving window are now covered by a dedicated check (§8.5). Fuse-saving scheme — POST /analysis/fuse-saving

The trip → switch → reconfigure loop

Protection chooses the device; switching is the result; OMS closes the loop — and three views of “dead” disagree on purpose.

fault→relay 51 senses via CT→operates the breaker (protection_trip)→device_state → OPEN→traces re-read live state→cone appears; OMS shows the dead region
  • Protection chooses the device; switching is the result. When it operates, the only thing that changes in the data is the breaker’s device_state.
  • A recloser does both in one feature — senses, trips (fast then slow), recloses, and locks out as device_state = OPEN.
  • OMS closes the loop for operators. As a switching order runs, each open/close step flips a device_state; the live model recomputes the incident’s dead cone. Its prediction engine blames the nearest protective device upstream of the calling customers — only MV_FUSE, MV_RECLOSER and MV_BREAKER count; an is_fused switch (FSW-12, the RMU tee ways) and a sectionalizer are skipped, and the class walk is strictly directional with no junction pass-through, so a customer fed through an RMU’s one-way bus can come back with no device (an unattributed cluster for a human to assign). Confidence = distinct calling service points ÷ service points below the device.

An option is feasible only if it restores load and causes no voltage violation (band 0.95–1.05 pu by default) and no overload (conductor or transformer loading above 100 %). It overrides one barrier per candidate and mutates nothing. It does not know where the fault is — with only REC-7 open, closing T-79 would “restore” the whole tail including the faulted C-2; isolate the fault first.

Back-feed capacity — how much headroom, not just “does it hold”. Each candidate now also reports how far from the limit the transfer runs (api/internal/powerflow/contingency.go:65-77): the worst loading % on the post-transfer donor path, the limiting element and which bound it hit (limiting_kind = ampacity for a conductor against its thermal rating, or transformer for a rated node against its kVA), and the spare apparent power at that element — limiting_headroom_kva = S · (100 − loading%)/loading%, negative when the transfer pushes it over. The donor’s loading before any transfer is reported too (base_worst_loading_pct). In the UI these are a Capacity column on the Contingency & backfeed panel (“87 % · +120 kVA · limited by MV_CONDUCTOR”, red past 100 %) — so closing T-79 tells you the back-feed trunk C-9 then sits at, say, 78 % with ~140 kVA to spare, a transfer you can hold. See Engineering Studies for the loading/ampacity math.

11.2 What the model does not do

ExpectationReality
“Opening a device re-routes power automatically”No. Only device_state changes; nothing re-feeds a region. A tie must be closed by a person or a switching step.
“Closing the tie clears the Live shading”No — a tie-fed region stays in the open device’s cone. Use the contingency study.
“The Live view shows what is unsupplied”It shows what is downstream of an open device, including the far side of every normally-open tie.
“The upstream boundary of an isolate is always an upstream device”Not guaranteed: the walk starts undirected and returns the first hit of an unordered set.
“A recloser counts its shots / a sectionalizer counts”No. reclose_attempts, reclose_interval_s, reset_time_s, count_to_lockout are stored, never simulated; lockout is whatever you set device_state to. (A sectionalizer does carry boundary_role = switching, functional_role = controller and tier 300 — it bounds sections; only the counting is un-simulated.)
“Coordination checks the recloser’s fast curve”The margin grader uses the slow curve only, at one current. The fast curve is checked separately by the fuse-saving study (§8.5).
“The protection-settings register feeds the grader”Not yet — coordination reads the relay feature’s own fields; the register (§7.4) is a parallel as-built record.
“The fuse is chosen or sized for me”No. fuse_rating_a and fuse_speed are labels; only curve_ref matters, and only one example fuse curve ships.
“CT ratio / saturation / burden is modelled”No. ct_ratio/vt_ratio are recorded; pickup is in primary amps.
“The relay’s other functions run”Only overcurrent — and device_number is never consulted, so the ANSI code neither enables nor restricts anything; protection_sensing is never read.
“A fault is simulated and the breaker trips”No time-domain simulation: a fault location is a question to the grader, not an event; device_state changes only by an edit or a switching step.
“Switchgear duty is compared with its rating”No kA rating is stored on the classes.
“Arc-flash includes breaker opening time”No; an instantaneous trip yields 0 cal/cm².
“Earthing is tracked”The earth switching step is a tick with no device; MV_EARTH_SWITCH is not on the connectivity graph.
“OMS blames a fused switch / counts every customer”Only MV_FUSE, MV_RECLOSER, MV_BREAKER; the class walk behind OMS prediction, “affected customers” and the switching order’s verify count is strictly directional (no junction pass-through), so a customer behind an RMU’s one-way bus can be missed or unattributed — while the energisation view (the cone) does include them.
“Interlocks / permit-to-work”Not modelled.
“Several relays on one breaker are combined”The first (oldest link) is graded, with a note.
“A curve can be added in the UI”No — SQL only.
“Power-flow engines honour every barrier rule”They keep one barrier rule per class (a map overwrite) and the rule loader has no ORDER BY: a class with both a device_state and an operational_state rule may be judged on only one in load flow / fault / coordination. Not verified at runtime — open a device, run a study across it, and expect the “not downstream of start” refusal.
