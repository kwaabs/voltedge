VoltEdge guide · outage management · from a customer’s call to a reliability index

Someone rings in.
Who is dark, and why?

OMS turns scattered trouble calls into incidents, predicts who is affected, drives the switching order that isolates and restores supply, and rolls the result up into reliability indices. This guide is a working copy of the real rules — JavaScript ports of the prediction engine, the customer attribution, the live de-energised cone, the switching-order generator, and the SAIDI/SAIFI queries — running on a small Feeder 7 estate in your browser, with the same status codes and error messages the Go handlers return.

Use As in the top bar. OMS has just two permissions, oms:read and oms:write. Priya (control room) and Dana (field crew) are editors: they hold both. Tom is an analyst and Sam a viewer: read only. Ada is an admin: both. Widgets lock to what the persona may do, and the endpoints tester returns the real 403.

Who this is for

Control-room operators who take calls and drive outages to restoration, planners scheduling planned work, and engineers reading reliability performance. The full manual (oms-guide.md) holds every table; this edition keeps all ten sections, the reference appendix and the corrections list, and makes each idea something you can poke.

Four honest limits up front, because they decide how far to trust a screen: “affected” and “dark now” are two different engines and are not meant to match (affected follows the normal supply tree and ignores live switch state; dark-now follows live state); prediction blames the nearest protective device on a call’s supply path — a fuse, recloser or breaker, or an is_fused switch — and escalates to the shared upstream device when calls arrive from several fuses; SAIDI/SAIFI/CAIDI are unplanned-only; and executing an open/close step really flips the device’s device_state. Chapters 4–6 and 8 show each of them with numbers.

01

What OMS is — five records, two hinges

OMS is its own schema (oms), not map features.

An outage is not something you draw. It is an operational transaction about the live network: a customer rang in, the engine blamed a protective device, N customers went dark, a crew restored it. So OMS owns hand-written tables (migration 0087; planned outages and switching orders in 0089; the interruption feature-class in 0119) and sits beside — never inside — the compiled gis.* feature tables. References to features (service_point_id, predicted_device_id, device_id) are bare ids with no foreign key: a feature purged later leaves a dangling id that the engines skip.

Five tables carry it. Click one to see its columns, constraints and who writes it. The two hinges to the rest of the system are dashed: a fault carries the incident_id and explains the outage; a defect / work order is reached only through the fault — an incident has no WAM column.

02

Who may do what

Two permissions, enforced method-aware on the whole /oms mount.

Migration 0088 seeds oms:read (“View the outage board…”) and oms:write (“Log trouble calls, run prediction, and confirm/restore/cancel incidents”). They are deliberately separate from features:write: a dispatcher can run the outage board without edit rights to assets, and a GIS editor can edit assets without touching live outages. The mount in main.go:266 is RequirePermission(PermOMSRead, PermOMSWrite): GET/HEAD/OPTIONS need read, everything else needs write (auth/rbac.go:75-80). No session is a 401, a missing permission a 403.

03

From trouble calls to incidents — prediction

Many small reports, one blamed device.

A trouble call (oms.trouble_calls) is one customer report: a call_type (no_supply / partial / flicker, default no_supply), a locator — a service-point id, an address or a map pin, at least one — and a status (open → associated → cleared / cancelled). ⚠️ Two corrections to the first edition: associating a call only fills in its service point — its status stays open until Predict links it to an incident — and nothing in the code ever sets cleared.

▶ Operator

How to — take and place a call 🔒 oms:write. Outages → Log call → type + locator → save (POST /oms/trouble-calls, status open). Then Associate: give the SP id, or pass snap = true to resolve the nearest service point to the call’s pin (POST /oms/trouble-calls/{id}/associate). An associated call has a feature to walk upstream from — only calls with a resolved service point feed prediction.

3.1 Prediction — the rules

Prediction rolls the open, SP-resolved calls into incidents (predict.go):

  • For each call’s service point it walks up its supply chain to the nearest protective device: ProtectiveDeviceClasses = {MV_FUSE, MV_RECLOSER, MV_BREAKER} or an MV_SWITCH whose is_fused flag is set (FusedSwitchFilters) — a fused switch such as FSW-12, or an RMU’s fused tee way, clears a fault the way a fuse does. A plain MV_SWITCH (a sectionaliser-style switch) is not protective, so the walk goes past it. (Earlier revisions blamed by class code only, so an is_fused switch was never blamed.)
  • Calls under the same device cluster into one incident; different devices give separate incidents; calls with no upstream device form a “no-device” cluster (its incident has a null confidence). Fuses escalate: a fused switch is the lowest-level protective device, so when calls arrive from several fused devices — or from a fused device and the non-fused device directly above it — the shared upstream device (the first non-fused protective device above them) is the one that operated, and the groups merge into it (clusterCalls). One fused device with calls and nothing else above it keeps the blame: one blown fuse. A human can name — or correct — the blamed device of a predicted or scheduled incident with POST /oms/incidents/{id}/attribute {device_class, device_id} (oms:write): the affected counts are recomputed, confidence is cleared and oms.incident.updated fires; it is refused once the incident is confirmed. There is no console button yet — call the endpoint.
  • A single-fault hypothesis: the nearest protective device is blamed, one fault per cluster. Nested faults are left to the operator to split or merge.
  • Confidence = distinct calling SPs ÷ the device’s affected SPs, capped at 1.0 (0 if the device has no downstream SPs): one call on a big feeder is weak, half the street calling is strong. A repeat call from the same SP changes nothing.
  • The walk uses the supply tree (trace/supply_tree.go, used by reachForOMS): the component is rooted at its grid infeed — the minimum-tier transformer/source nodes, e.g. POWER_XFMR — and a breadth-first spanning tree is grown outward over the undirected connectivity, stepping only to an equal-or-higher trace_tier (never back toward the source) and not crossing a device whose normal state is open (normal_state = NORMALLY_OPEN, e.g. the tie T-79 — reached, not crossed). A node’s parent is the neighbour it is normally fed from, so “upstream of a call” is its parent chain and “below a device” is that device’s subtree, whichever way the ways of an RMU were wired. It ignores live switch state and has no barriers. Without a usable root (no tiers seeded, or a component whose top tier is not a transformer/source) it falls back to a junction-aware walk with a locked start. A ring closed in normal state is a mesh: a node takes the first parent the search reaches (ties by class and id), so its subtree is deterministic but arbitrary between the two feeds. (Earlier revisions used a strictly directed walk that ran out of arrows at an RMU bus; fixed, covered by unit tests on an in-memory Feeder 7 graph, not yet run against a live database.)
▶ Operator

How to — predict incidents 🔒 oms:write. Outages → Predict (POST /oms/incidents/predict). Open, SP-resolved calls roll into predicted incidents, each blamed on a device with affected counts and a confidence; the calls become associated and linked. It touches only status = open calls, so re-running picks up new calls without duplicating incidents. It fires oms.incident.raised per incident, and writes nothing to the live network.

04

The incident & who’s affected

One status machine, one frozen set, one honest caveat.

An incident (oms.incidents) is the outage record: the predicted_device_class/_id it’s blamed on, a circuit_id reporting axis, an outage_type (unplanned/planned), a free-text cause_code, a confidence, timings (started_at, etr, restored_at), the denormalised affected_customers / affected_service_points, and crew_ref / notes (free text).

  unplanned:  predicted ──► confirmed ──► restored        (or ──► cancelled)
  planned:    scheduled ──► confirmed ──► restored         (confirmed = executed)
  • predicted — just rolled up from calls; affected counts are live estimates.
  • confirmed — the operator confirms; this freezes the affected set into customer_interruptions rows. An unplanned incident’s interruption clock starts at the incident’s started_at (the moment prediction created it), not at the confirm click; a planned outage’s starts at confirm. An incident with no device freezes nothing.
  • restored — supply’s back; restored_at stamped, interruption windows closed. (The API also lets you restore a predicted or scheduled incident directly — only restored/cancelled are refused.)
  • cancelled — a false alarm; linked calls go back to open. Interruption rows already frozen stay, but are excluded from the indices.
  • dispatched is defined and allowed by the CHECK constraint, but nothing transitions to it.

4.1 Who’s affected — what counts as a customer supply

The affected rollup walks downstream of the blamed device and sums supplies through one shared predicate in the trace package (DownstreamLoadLeaves), so OMS “affected”, the frozen interruptions and a downstream service trace can never drift. Two supply classes count: an unmetered SERVICE_POINT, and a METER only when meter_role = PRIMARY (a CHECK or role-unset meter contributes nothing, so load is counted once). A null — or zero — customers_served reads as 1. A load_priority that is unset or invalid lands in P4; P1–P2 are critical. The rollup splits customers and load (kW) by tier, which is how the console says “N critical customers off”.

4.2 What “below the device” means — a junction-aware, barrier-free walk

The affected rollup is barrier-free and topological: it ignores live switch state, which is right for reliability (SAIDI/SAIFI shouldn’t shrink because a tie happens to be closed). “Below the device” is the device’s subtree of the normal supply tree (trace/supply_tree.go, trace/service.go:432-445): the component is rooted at its grid infeed, a spanning tree is grown over the undirected connectivity (never stepping back to a lower trace_tier), and a device whose normal state is open (normal_state = NORMALLY_OPEN, e.g. the tie T-79) is reached but not crossed. So the RMU’s ways, wired into the bus (way.T2 → bus), no longer matter: REC-7 counts 2 service points, 155 customers (SP-220 and the clinic SP-230, the same as the live cone), Way T counts just the clinic’s 70 customers, Way B feeds only the open tail (0), and Feeder 9 is never dragged in. The same set feeds three numbers: the incident’s affected_customers, the frozen interruptions (and so SAIDI), and the switching order’s “Verify N supplies”. (The first edition’s walk was strictly directed and stopped at the RMU bus, missing the clinic; a first fix that passed through junctions without a tree flooded through ties and back up the feeder; the tree replaced both. Unit-tested on an in-memory graph, not run against a live database.) Because it follows normal state it can still count supplies that are dark or back-fed right now in a different way — a device that opened below, a closed tie — which the live cone (next chapter) accounts for.

05

The live de-energised cone

What is dark right now, given the switch positions.

GET /oms/incidents/{id}/energization answers “what is dark right now?” (energization.go). It reads the blamed device’s live device_state: if the device is not OPEN (or models no device_state, or has been purged) the set is empty — supply is on. If it is OPEN, trace.DeEnergizedCone(device) — a barrier-respecting walk from each of the device’s downstream neighbours, with the engine’s junction pass-through — returns the dead set, rolled up by tier (including critical customers off). It shares its graph code with the Switching & Protection guide.

The cone is live: open the device and it grows; close it and it collapses to empty; open a further device below it (a sectionalising or back-feed step) and it shrinks to the still-isolated part. That is what drives the red shading on the outage map. Click a device to toggle it; choose which device the incident blames.

06

Switching orders — and the live-state coupling

The program a crew ticks off; each tick can move the real model.

A switching order (oms.switching_orders) is the ordered program a crew executes to isolate the incident and later restore it. Its steps (oms.switching_steps) each carry a phase (isolate/restore), an action (open/close/earth/verify/note), the target device_class/_id, an instruction, and an executed_at (null = outstanding).

▶ Operator

How to — generate a switching order 🔒 oms:write. Open the incident → Generate switching order (POST /oms/incidents/{id}/switching-orders, 201). It builds a draft 5-step program around the blamed device:

  ISOLATE   1. Open    the blamed device
            2. Verify  N supplies de-energised   (N = the junction-aware downstream supply count, Chapter 4)
            3. Earth   at the nearest upstream isolation boundary (or at the device if none)
  RESTORE   4. Note    "remove earths"
            5. Close   the blamed device

The upstream boundary is the nearest hit among SwitchingDeviceClasses = {MV_SWITCH, MV_ISOLATOR, MV_SECTIONALIZER, MV_RECLOSER, MV_BREAKER} — the sectionaliser is a valid isolation point — found by the same junction-aware class walk, and never the blamed device itself. It requires an attributed device (a no-device incident is a 400 — attribute it first with POST /incidents/{id}/attribute), an incident that is not restored/cancelled, and no existing live order (cancel it first).

▶ Operator

How to — execute a step 🔒 oms:write. Tick a step (POST /oms/switching-steps/{id}/execute, body {executed?} defaults to true). The order’s status rolls up: all steps executed → completed, some → in_progress, none → issued (a cancelled order is never resurrected).

Interactive · 9

Order generator — and what ticking does to the network

⚠️ Executing an open/close step changes the real network. Ticking an open or close step flips the target device’s live device_state — through crud.UpdateFeature, so it is validated and logged to gis.feature_history with the acting user — and every trace, the power-flow engine and the map immediately reflect it. verify, earth and note steps only record executed_at. A device with no device_state, or one that has been deleted, is silently skipped — the step never fails. Un-ticking writes the opposite position, not the previous one (switching.go:322-334): un-tick open → CLOSED; un-tick close → OPEN. So with REC-7 already locked out, ticking its “open” step is a no-op but un-ticking it closes REC-7 in the model. Try it above.

So as Dana ticks the isolate and restore steps, Priya watches the red cone on the outage map shrink in real time — the steps are operating the model, not just checking boxes. Cancelling an order keeps its steps but does not undo any state it flipped, and a completed order cannot be cancelled.

07

Planned outages

Not every outage is a fault.

A planned outage is scheduled around a device for maintenance. How to — schedule one 🔒 oms:write: Outages → Plan outage (POST /oms/incidents/planned, 201): pick the device, set planned_start / planned_end (start must be before end). It creates a scheduled, outage_type = planned incident whose affected set is computed the same (junction-aware) way and fires oms.incident.raised. When the work starts, confirm it (scheduled → confirmed resets started_at to now and freezes the interruptions with that clock); restore it when supply’s back. Planned incidents appear on the board exactly like unplanned ones, flagged by outage_type — but they are excluded from SAIDI/SAIFI/CAIDI and ENS (IEEE 1366 convention, indices.go:213); the first edition said otherwise.

08

Restore, and reliability indices

Stamp the end, close the windows, roll the facts up.

Restore (POST /oms/incidents/{id}/restore) stamps restored_at, closes every open interruption window the incident owns, and fires oms.incident.restored; it is refused only for an already-restored or cancelled incident. Cancel (POST …/cancel) voids a false incident and releases its calls — but a restored incident cannot be cancelled. Chapter 4’s status machine lets you try each.

Reliability indices (GET /oms/indices?from=&to=&avg_load_kw=&circuit_id=, indices.go) compute the IEEE 1366-style metrics over a window from the frozen customer_interruptions of unplanned, non-cancelled incidents. The default window is the trailing 12 months; from must be before to and avg_load_kw must not be negative (both 400).

IndexFormulaUnit
SAIFIcustomers interrupted ÷ Ninterruptions / customer
SAIDIcustomer-minutes ÷ Nminutes / customer
CAIDIcustomer-minutes ÷ customers interrupted (= SAIDI ÷ SAIFI; 0 if none)minutes / interruption
ENScustomer-minutes ÷ 60 × avg_load_kw (default 1 kW; flagged estimated)kWh
  • N = customers served on the live system: Σ COALESCE(customers_served,1) over service points plus PRIMARY meters (whole-system report). A circuit_id-scoped report stays service-point-only on both sides, because meters carry no circuit yet (and needs gis.service_point.circuit_id).
  • Clipping. Each interruption is clipped to the window; a still-open one counts up to to. It counts toward SAIFI (frequency) only if it began in the window — a carried-over outage inflates duration but not frequency.
  • Cancelled incidents’ and planned outages’ interruptions are excluded. MAIFI (momentary) is not computed.

By fault cause — buckets that sum back to the system figure

∑ The one place OMS reads Faults. The indices response carries by_fault_cause: every in-window interruption lands in exactly one bucket — its incident’s fault cause (one representative fault per incident: earliest detected_at), an uncategorised fault (cause null, fault_linked true), or “no fault linked” — over the same N, so the buckets’ SAIDI/SAIFI sum to the headline. 09

Integration

Webhooks out, tools in, and the two hinges.

  • Outbound webhooks. oms.incident.raised fires on predict (one per incident) and on planned-create; oms.incident.restored on restore. oms.incident.updated fires only on POST /oms/incidents/{id}/attribute — confirm, cancel and switching are silent. Wire these to a downstream CIS/OMS mirror. Mechanics (from webhookout): the event is a row in a transactional outbox written inside the write’s own transaction, so a rolled-back change leaves no event and a committed one always does; the separate webhookworker process fans each out to subscriptions and POSTs a signed envelope (headers X-VoltEdge-Event, -Delivery — the idempotency key, -Timestamp, -Signature: sha256=… HMAC). Delivery is therefore at-least-once. (Planned-create inserts the incident and emits as two statements, not one transaction.)
  • Agent / MCP tools (api/internal/agenttools/tools_oms.go) — the whole console is agent-callable: 19 tools (9 reads, 10 writes), each carrying its route permission; the ten writes are annotated “confirm with a human”.
  • The fault & defect hinges — raise a fault from an incident (→ Faults guide), which in turn books a WAM defect (→ Works, Inspections & Maintenance). OMS never touches WAM directly.

Worked example — a Feeder 7 outage, call to restoration

Eight beats, run on the real rules — including where the first edition’s story was too tidy.

A persistent fault on the Feeder 7 main; REC-7 does its reclose sequence and locks out OPEN. Everything below it — the FSW-12/TX-220 tee, RMU-5/TX-230, the tail — goes dark. Step through it: each button runs the real handler (same status codes, same numbers) against the estate; the clock, the graph and the ledger on the right update. Interactive · 14

Feeder 7 — call to restoration

  1. The lock-out and the calls. REC-7 trips OPEN. Several customers ring in no_supply; Priya logs each (or they arrive via IVR) and associates them to service points — snap = true resolves the nearest service point from the pin.
  2. Predict. The call from SP-220 walks up its supply chain (TX-220, C-3, FSW-12 — a fused MV_SWITCH is protective — then C-2, REC-7); on its own it would blame the nearest protective device, FSW-12 (1 service point, 85 customers, confidence 1.0). The clinic’s call (SP-230, fed through RMU-5’s fused tee way WT) blames WT. Two fused groups sit under the same upstream device, so they escalate: both calls fold into one incident blamed on REC-7. confidence = calling SPs ÷ service points below the device = 2 ÷ 2 = 1.0 (one call alone would have stayed on its fuse).
  3. Review who’s out. The affected panel lists SP-220 and the clinic SP-230 (P2, critical) — 2 service points, 155 customers, the same set the map’s live cone shows with REC-7 OPEN: 2 service points, 155 customers, 570 kW, 1 critical SP.
  4. Confirm. Priya confirms; the affected set freezes into customer_interruptions — the SAIDI clock for those rows runs from the incident’s started_at.
  5. Switching order. She generates one: isolate [open REC-7 — already open; verify 1 supply dead; earth at the upstream boundary CB-7] → restore [remove earths; close REC-7]. Dana ticks the isolate steps; each open/close tick flips the live state. To restore the healthy tail early Priya opens Way A and FSW-12 (cutting off the faulted C-2) and closes the normal-open tie T-79 to back-feed from Feeder 9 — manual operations beyond the auto order (Switching & Protection guide). The cone around REC-7 shrinks to C-2 and its two open neighbours — the clinic is back on supply, and SP-220 (now behind the open FSW-12) also drops out of REC-7’s cone even though it is still without supply: the cone is “the part isolated by the blamed device”, and what lies beyond another open device belongs to that device’s cone.
  6. Repair. From the incident Priya raises a fault (it inherits REC-7), the crew finds the cause, and books a defect off that fault → a WAM work order (Faults / WAM guides).
  7. Restore & close. Repair done, Dana closes REC-7 (ticking the restore step flips it CLOSED); the cone collapses to empty. Priya restores the incident — restored_at stamped, interruption windows closed.
  8. Reliability. Tom pulls GET /oms/indices: this outage’s frozen interruptions contribute to SAIDI/SAIFI, and by_fault_cause attributes it to the dig_in recorded on the fault. The clinic’s 70 customers are in no interruption row, so the index understates this outage.
