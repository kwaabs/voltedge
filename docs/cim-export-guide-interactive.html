Not a map.
A network model.

CIM export turns the features your crews survey into the language utility engineering software speaks: IEC 61970/61968 Common Information Model, serialised as RDF/XML. This guide is a working copy of the real encoder — a JavaScript port that, for the Oakwood sample, produces the identical bytes the server does. Change a mapping, cut a connection, tick a class, and watch the file change.

Use As in the top bar. Priya (planner) holds features:read — she can export. Tom adds metadata:read and can open the mapping editor read-only. Ada adds metadata:write and can change mappings. Dana has no access to either. Widgets lock to what the persona may do and tell you what is missing.

Who this is for

Planners and network engineers who need the model in a power-flow or planning tool; GIS and asset administrators who maintain the mapping; and integrators who receive the file. The full manual (cim-export-guide.md) holds every table, SQL recipe and appendix; this edition keeps all ten chapters and makes each idea something you can poke.

Three honest limits up front, because they decide whether the file suits your tool: units are SI base units (volts, VA, metres, ohms); there is no load, set-point or switch-position data beyond normalOpen; and the file is a single CDPSM-style document, not a CGMES EQ/TP/SSH/SV split. Chapter 6 lists eighteen such limits with workarounds, Chapter 8 shows how to bridge them.

01

What CIM is, and why export it

A model, not a map — and where the export sits among the others.

CIM — the Common Information Model — is the IEC’s vocabulary for electric utilities: IEC 61970 (energy management: Terminal, ConnectivityNode, ACLineSegment…), IEC 61968 (distribution management: assets, metering, work) and IEC 62325 (markets). It defines what things are and how they connect, independent of any drawing. A power-flow tool cannot do anything useful with a polyline; it needs to know that this conductor’s end 2 meets that transformer’s primary terminal at one electrical node.

The export therefore does three things your KMZ or Shapefile does not: it gives every feature a CIM class (a line segment, a load-break switch, a power transformer); it rebuilds the topology (terminals and connectivity nodes) from the connections your crews captured; and it writes the attributes engineers need in the units CIM tools expect (voltage, rating, length, impedance). Click through the pipeline to see which stage does what — and where each can fail.

02

The mental model

Three graphs, a chain from feature to node, stable ids, and a scope that closes the graph.

A VoltEdge network is really three graphs sharing the same features. The spatial one (where things are) is what the map shows. The connectivity one (which feature touches which, via which terminal) is what crews capture. The containment one (this switch lives in that substation) is optional. CIM cares most about the second: it needs a chain from each feature to an electrical node.

2.2 Identity — ids that survive re-export

  
Every equipment element carries the feature’s own UUID as its rdf:ID="_uuid" and again as the IdentifiedObject.mRID literal. Re-export tomorrow and the same line is the same line — which is what lets a recipient diff two exports. Everything derived (a Location, a Terminal, a BaseVoltage) gets a deterministic UUIDv5 (name-based, SHA-1) under one frozen namespace, so it too is repeatable. The lab computes them live; the namespace and name formats are exactly the encoder’s.

03
The declarative mapping
Three tables decide everything — no code change, no redeploy.

  
What a feature becomes is data, held in three tables under metadata and edited in Admin → CIM mappings (🔒 metadata:write to change, metadata:read to look):

  

    
TableOne row says…Key columns

    
cim_profilesthe export target: namespace, profile URI, authority, CRS, and the unmapped-attribute policy (drop or carry)code, cim_namespace, profile_uri, unmapped_attr_policy, is_default

    
cim_class_mappingsfeature class (+ optional subtype) → CIM class and kindfeature_class_id, subtype_id, cim_class, cim_kind

    
cim_attribute_mappingsa field → a CIM attribute through one of seven transformsfield_id, cim_attribute, transform, scale, value_map, unit

  
The kind is what drives structure: conducting_edge (a line — two end terminals), conducting_junction (a device — its layer terminals), container (substation-like; carries a location, anchors other equipment), asset (a pole or meter — no terminals), protection (a relay) and skip (a recorded decision not to export).

04
Topology
From pairwise connections to terminals and nodes.

  
Crews capture connectivity as pairs: “conductor A connects to transformer terminal T1”. CIM wants nodes: the set of terminals that are electrically one point. The encoder builds them with union-find over endpoint keys — T|feature|terminal for a device terminal, E|feature|0/1 for the two ends of a conductor — then writes one ConnectivityNode per set and one Terminal per key.

4.2 Which end of a conductor? — the geometry heuristic

  
A conductor connection usually names no terminal. The encoder picks the conductor’s end nearest the other feature (squared planar distance, strict “closer” — a tie goes to end 0). For two conductors it picks the closest pair of ends. It is a heuristic: it is right when features are drawn end-to-end and wrong when they are not. Drag the neighbour and see the choice flip.

  

    
Interactive · 9
Nearest-end disambiguation

    

    

  

  
When both of a conductor’s ends are closest to the same neighbour, or a neighbour has no geometry, the heuristic can attach the wrong end or fall back to end 0 — and nothing complains. The validator’s E-452 (both ends on one node) and “floating end” warnings are how you find these (Chapter 9).


  
4.3 Complete terminal sets, and transformer windings

  
Every conducting feature gets a complete terminal set, even when no connection mentions it. A conductor always has two. A device has one per non-ground layer terminal (or one implicit terminal if the layer defines none). A PowerTransformer is padded to two windings, because CIM requires an HV and an LV end. Each winding becomes a PowerTransformerEnd with its rated voltage and the shared rating.

4.4 Phasing — one code, several objects

  
A coded phase value becomes a set of child objects, one per phase, each with a SinglePhaseKind and a reference to its parent. Conductors get ACLineSegmentPhase; street lights get EnergyConsumerPhase. Transformers, busbars and meters are unphased, and Terminal.phases is never written (gap G6).

4.5 Containers

  
A node and a piece of equipment each get a container: a modelled container (an explicit containment relationship) wins; otherwise the encoder tests whether the feature’s first point lies inside a substation polygon; otherwise the catch-all Line named “Unassigned equipment”. In Oakwood the switch sits inside the substation polygon and everything else is “Unassigned”. See it in the next widget’s counts, then in the file.

05
Running an export & reading the file
Request, job, download — then a tour of what came out.

  
Exports are asynchronous jobs. The web app (or the API) creates a row in ops.export_jobs; a separate process, cmd/exportworker, claims it, encodes the file, uploads it to object storage and marks the job succeeded. You then download through a presigned link valid for 15 minutes; jobs and files are kept 7 days. Any authenticated user with features:read can export what they can see (territory scope and private drafts apply).

07
The standard, in practice
What CIM, CIMXML and the profiles actually say — and where VoltEdge follows or chooses.

  
“CIM” is not one document. It is a family: IEC 61970-301 is the base schema (the classes and attributes, a UML model); IEC 61968-11 extends it for distribution assets, metering and work; IEC 62325-301 covers markets. A profile selects a subset for one exchange job — IEC 61970-452 (the equipment/topology profiles used for network models), IEC 61970-600 (the European CGMES), IEC 61968-13 (CDPSM, the distribution profile VoltEdge targets). A serialisation is the file syntax: IEC 61970-552 defines CIMXML, an RDF/XML dialect. Three layers — schema, profile, serialisation — and mixing them up is the commonest source of confusion.

A note on what this guide has and has not verified. IEC standards are paywalled and the build environment could not fetch most external pages, so the statements about the standards come from published summaries, open-source tool documentation and search snippets, not from reading the full text. Attribute-level units and the exact profile identifiers should be confirmed against the profile your recipient’s tool targets before you rely on them.

7.2 Node-breaker or bus-branch? — switch state decides the nodes

  
VoltEdge writes node-breaker detail: every terminal, every connectivity node, switches as equipment. A consumer builds bus-branch “topological nodes” by merging nodes through closed switches. Flip the switch and watch the consumer’s node count change — which is why Switch.normalOpen is the one state value VoltEdge does carry (G4/G5).

09
Validate, diff and repair
Three levels of “is it right?” — and a validator you can break.

  
Level 1 — well-formed: it parses as XML. Level 2 — structurally valid: ids unique, references resolve, equipment has the right terminals on distinct nodes, transformers have two windings. Level 3 — electrically sensible: voltages agree across a node, units look right, lines have length. VoltEdge guarantees level 1 and most of 2 by construction; level 3 depends on your data, which is why scripts/cim/cim_validate.py exists. The widget below runs the same checks in your browser against the live Oakwood file, and lets you inject the defects it is designed to catch.

Exit codes matter in a pipeline: errors (E-…) fail the run, warnings (W-…) do not unless --strict. W-OPEN on a fresh export is normal — every feeder end and every unconnected switch terminal is a legitimate single-terminal node. Compare it with your own list of intentional ends.


  
9.1 Diffing two exports

  
Because equipment and terminal ids are stable (Chapter 2), a diff between yesterday’s and today’s export shows real change. Strip the volatile header (Model.created, the FullModel id), then diff. Node ids will differ wherever connectivity changed — expected. The encoder is deterministic: same data and same mapping give the same bytes except for that header, which the test suite asserts.
