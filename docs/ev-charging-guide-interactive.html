01

The two numbers of an EV load

Installed nameplate is not what the grid sees.

An EV charging site has two very different load figures, and confusing them is the number-one planning mistake:

Installed nameplateadd up every charger's rating. 8 × 150 kW DC fast = 1,200 kW installed.
vs
What the grid seesthe coincident draw: diversified, then held under a managed cap. Often a fraction of nameplate.

The grid is sized for the second number. A site can have 1,200 kW of chargers installed and present 250 kW to the feeder, because its energy-management system caps coincident draw. VoltEdge models exactly this: you record the nameplate, the diversity, and the cap, and every analysis uses the effective demand those three produce (Chapter 3).

▶ Why it matters at the keyboard

Size a feeder against installed nameplate and you over-build; ignore the cap and you reject viable connections. The model exists so the real coincident load rides into trace, load flow, and the headroom study automatically — you enter three fields, the maths does the rest.

02

The data model

Station → charger → connector. Only the station is an electrical node.

EV charging ships as a metadata seed (seed/ev_charging_example.sql, Phase 54) — not a migration. Three classes in a containment hierarchy, but only the station wires into the network.

The station's fields

The charger (charger_type ac_l2dc_fastdc_ultra, rated_power_kw, num_connectors, managed) and connector (connector_standard type2ccs2chademonacsgbt, rated_power_kw) record the equipment, but carry no load into the network themselves.

∑ Honest caveat — the roll-up is hand-maintained

connected_load_kw is a denormalised attribute stored directly on the station, read straight off its own row in the hot sweep (no containment walk). There is no trigger or aggregation code that sums the contained chargers' rated_power_kw into it — you set connected_load_kw by hand. The charger/connector features are inventory you attach for completeness; today they do not auto-compute the station's connected load. The whole domain is metadata seed — no migration.

03

How the load is sized — managed charging

Three fields collapse to one effective demand.

The whole model reduces to one formula, applied in resolveDemandKW (api/internal/powerflow/resolve.go) — the single point every analysis reads demand through:

effective_kw = min( managed_demand_cap_kw , connected_load_kw × diversity_factor )

∑ Exactly how it resolves
  • EV base: if connected_load_kw is present, base = connected_load_kw × diversity_factor — and it takes precedence over any demand_kw on the same feature.
  • Ordinary load: no connected_load_kw → base = demand_kw. Plain SERVICE_POINTs are unaffected.
  • diversity_factor defaults to 1.0 when absent, ≤ 0, or > 1.
  • The cap clamps: if managed_demand_cap_kw is present and > 0 and the base exceeds it, the base is clamped to it. No cap if the field is absent or ≤ 0.
  • Reactive power: Q = P · tan(acos(pf)), pf defaulting to unity (Q = 0) when unset or outside (0, 1].
04

How it loads the network

A leaf load node that sags the feeder as it grows.

▶ Once modelled, an EV_CHARGING_STATION behaves like any other load. Wire it to the transformer secondary and every study that walks the feeder carries its effective demand (Chapter 3) back to the source.

Grow the load and the far end sags toward the 0.95 pu floor. The question is: how much can it grow first? → Chapter 5.

05

The feeder-headroom study

How much more charging load before the first limit binds?

The study (POST /analysis/ev-hosting, ev_hosting.go) is the exact under-voltage mirror of DER hosting capacity: hosting injects generation and binds on over-voltage; EV headroom adds load and binds on under-voltage (the 0.95 pu floor) — or on thermal (a conductor/transformer at 100 % of rating), which binds identically in both.

Binding by margin: under-voltage worst_voltage_pu − 0.95 vs thermal 1 − worst_loading_pct/100 — whichever is smaller is the limit the next kW crosses. Reach the 100 MW cap still feasible → unbounded, binding = none. The feeder is resolved once, then every trial is a pure in-memory re-sweep.

▶ The inputs that matter
InputDefaultEffect
nominal_voltage_v— requiredthe feeder slack voltage — must match the feeder's real voltage
power_factor1.0pf < 1 draws kVAR, deepening the drop, lowering headroom

⚠ The nominal-voltage gotcha. The panel defaults nominal_voltage_v to 400 V. Arm it on an 11 kV / MV feeder and leave 400 V, and the per-unit maths is against the wrong base — the headroom is meaningless. Always enter the feeder's actual sending-end voltage.

06

Reading & acting on the result

The binding constraint tells you what to do.

Each connection node reports its ev_hosting_capacity_kw (the headroom), the binding_constraint, the binding_feature it acts on, and the worst_voltage_pu / worst_loading_pct at that capacity — so you can see how close each limit sits. The panel's “Binds on” and “At capacity” columns are exactly this.

Binds onMeansLever
Under-voltagethe far end would fall below 0.95 puraise the source/tap, reconductor, or accept a lower floor
Thermala conductor/transformer hits 100 % of ratingupgrade that specific element (it's named)
Pre-existingthe feeder already violates a limit (headroom 0)fix the base case first — it isn't the EV's fault
Unboundednothing binds up to the 100 MW capheadroom is effectively unlimited here

The number is only half the answer. The binding constraint is the other half — it names the bottleneck and, with it, the fix.

07

Planning with managed charging

Install more than the headroom — and stay within it.

The headroom the study reports is the uncapped physical ceiling. Managed charging is the lever that lets you connect a site whose installed nameplate exceeds it: you compare the site's managed effective load (Chapter 3) against the headroom (Chapter 5), and tune the cap until they fit.

You don't size the feeder for the chargers. You size the cap for the feeder — and install whatever nameplate you like behind it.
08

Worked example — the Oakwood LV board

End to end, with the real numbers.

Tom, a network analyst, sizes the rapid-charging hub at the Oakwood LV board (415 V node off the Feeder-7 transformer, base voltage 0.969 pu with the feeder's existing load). He runs /analysis/ev-hosting naming that node, unity pf, floor 0.95. The search adds load and re-sweeps:

Added loadWorst voltageFeasible?
50 kW0.966 pu✅
100 kW0.962 pu✅
200 kW0.954 pu✅
400 kW0.943 pu ⚠️❌ under-voltage

Bisection pins the capacity between 200 (feasible) and 400 (infeasible):

ev_hosting_capacity_kw ≈ 250 kW
worst_voltage_pu     = 0.950  (exactly on the floor)
worst_loading_pct    = 71 %   (thermal has margin to spare)
binding_constraint   = undervoltage
▶ Tom's read

The board can take ~250 kW of charging before it hits the 0.95 pu floor; thermal isn't the problem (71 %). This board is voltage-limited — a tap or reconductor would raise it. (Contrast: the same board hosts ~480 kW of PV generation before over-voltage binds — generation and load pull voltage opposite ways.) To connect the operator's 500 kW hub, Tom sets a 240 kW managed cap (Chapter 7) and it fits.
